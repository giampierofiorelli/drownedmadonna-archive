Kicked out of Canada for Thanksgiving
When a young gay American heads across the border to share Canadian Thanksgiving with his boyfriend in Toronto, his past comes back to haunt him—and he winds up barred from the country.
By Justin Bain

An Advocate.com exclusive posted November 25, 2003

I am 23 years old and live in Buffalo, N.Y., and my boyfriend lives in Toronto. He is 21 and a student at Ryerson University studying fashion design and technology. I work for a large law firm here and plan on going back to school to finish my undergraduate work, and then—off to law school.

My boyfriend and I met a couple of months back. Actually, we met in the strangest way. On three separate occasions, we ran into each other in our respective cities. After that, we figured that there was a reason that we kept crossing paths. I think it haû something to do with Mars being in alignment with Uranus, and we got drops of Jupiter in our hair… Well, you get the picture.We started talking on a regular basis on the phone and, of course, after seeing the phone bill, used our online resources.

As you may know, Buffalo is a border city to Canada. No lie: I can throw a stone across the river and hit a Canuck. Anyway—a couple of weekends ago I was making my weekly visit to Toronto to visit the boy. It was a Friday and a special weekend because it was Canada’s Thanksgiving holiday. (Which, by the way, I don’t know what they are giving thanks for exactly. I’m still trying to figure out if the Pilgrims landed there first.) It was my usual routine: I catch the bus and then we spend an hour at the border going through customs. Little did I know this weekend was going to be different.

As I was getting questioned by the customs agent—the standard “Where are you going? Why? For how long?”—we got to the part about going to see my boyfriend, and the conversation went a whole different direction. I knew, as soon as that word came out of my mouth that I was in for trouble. The usual two or three questions turned into about 10 minutes. The customs guy asked if I had ever been in any trouble with the law. That’s where it got complicated.

For those of you who don’t know, Canada does not let anyone with a criminal record into the country. But when the agent asked if I had been in any trouble, Of course, I had to tell them about my little brush with the law when I was a teenager. At that point our “friendly neighbor” became the nasty dog next door—or, if you’re in a gay bar, the big drag queen that you always avoid. The agent sent me to immigration, and they sent me packing. This was by far the shortest stay I had ever had in Canada.

Let me elaborate a little on “my trouble.” When I was 17, as with most gay boys, I was experimenting with other guys. Well, my best friend at the time and I had a little romp in the hay. Nothing major: He showed me his, I showed him mine, I touched his, and—well, you get the picture. We stayed best friends, and it was as if nothing had happened. Then about a year later, just after my 18th birthday, I found out that he was going around telling people what had happened. The catch here is a double one: He was 14 at the time of our little encounter, now 15, and also he wasn’t coming out of the closet. Oh, no, he was saying that I forced him to do it.

Long story short, the whole thing turned into a Clintonesque scandal. The big difference was, I didn’t have the money that Clinton did, and in the end I was sent to jail for four months and given five years probation.

Let me tell you something: Jail is not the best place for an 18-year-old gay boy from the suburbs. But I digress…

I have since gotten over the horrors of my early adult life. (And they wonder why people end up in bell towers with high-powered rifles.) Fortunately, there are a couple of good things that came out of this. One, I wasn’t charged with a sex crime, so no registering or anything like that. Two, I have embraced my sexuality, as well as the community that I belong to. And three—make that three things—I realized how homophobic this world really is, if that’s a good thing.

But there is a happy ending, sort of.

I called my boyfriend from the border to tell him the bad news. There I was, standing in a foreign country, about to be expelled, and what does my boyfriend do? He tells me that he will call me back in 20 minutes. I didn’t know whether to laugh or cry.

After proceeding into more friendly territory—U.S. Customs—I got the call. He was at the Greyhound station in Toronto, catching a bus to Buffalo, so that he could spend the weekend with me. I just melted, and at 3:30 a.m. I picked him up from the bus station with the biggest smile I think I have ever had. It was probably the sweetest thing that anyone had ever done for me.

We had a great weekend, and his mother even froze some Thanksgiving dinner for me.

The final part of this story is not so great. I cannot reenter Canada until I fill out a mountain of paperwork. And of course there is a cost for all of this: $295, nonrefundable (and that’s American dollars, damn it). Even worse, there is no guarantee that this six-week process will be approved.

So you can obviously see my dilemma. I don’t really know why I wrote this; maybe to vent, or maybe in the hopes that someone will have some fabulous “gay mom” advice. The one thing that I know is that I love my boyfriend very much and I am going to try everything that I can to be with him. Talk about being worlds apart!

As they say, love knows no bounds. If you think that you can help, I would love to hear your ideas. I am working every political, legal, general contact that I know, but it never hurts to ask for help.
