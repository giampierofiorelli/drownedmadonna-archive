Chicago Tribune: EMI may acquire Warner Music.

EMI Group PLC, home to old-time rockers the Rolling Stones and jazz star Norah Jones, said on Monday it had opened formal talks to buy AOL Time Warner Inc.'s music business in a deal that could be worth more than $1.5 billion.

An EMI-Warner Music combination would create a music giant second only to Universal Music, with a roster spanning The Beatles, Frank Sinatra, Madonna and Christina Aguilera, as the industry scrambles to find a quick fix to sliding music sales.

"Discussions are at a very preliminary stage, and there is no assurance that they will result in an agreement acceptable to both parties," EMI said, confirming Reuters reports that EMI was courting Time Warner as rival talks with Bertelsmann faltered.

EMI and Warner Music, the world's third- and fourth-biggest music companies, tried to merge three years ago but failed after stumbling at the regulatory gate.

With sales sliding, the music companies hope regulators will be more lenient this time around, but independent labels said they will lobby against a deal.

Sources familiar with the situation told Reuters last week that EMI had refreshed a bid it made for Warner Music earlier this year, which included $1 billion of cash and would give AOL Time Warner a 25 percent stake in a combination.

London-based EMI's shares ended 4.1 percent lower, adding to an initial decline amid uncertainty over the outcome of the Warner talks, after Standard & Poor's placed the company's ratings on CreditWatch with negative implications.

"This can only be encouraging for EMI investors, as it offers the prospect of cost savings. But we'll have to wait and see how much they end up paying," said Investec analyst Kingsley Wilson.

AOL Time Warner, soon to return to its original name Time Warner, decided last week to open the field of contenders for Warner Music after struggling to wrap up a deal with German rival Bertelsmann, people familiar with the situation said.

However, talks between AOL Time Warner and Bertelsmann over a 50-50 recorded music joint venture are continuing.

"The talks have lost momentum, but the negotiations are far from dead," said one source familiar with the talks.

AOL Time Warner and Bertelsmann declined to comment.

Facing a gloomy outlook, neither Warner Music, EMI nor Bertelsmann's music arm BMG wants to be left out in the next round of consolidation as each attempts to slash costs.

AOL Time Warner, which has already sold its CD and DVD manufacturing business, also has been under pressure to pare its long-term debt, which stood at $26 billion as of June 30.

However, getting a music merger through regulators remains a big hurdle, and independent labels said they would oppose a deal.

What if Madonna had kissed Pink? It almost happened.
It was a moment that captivated the nation, but what would it have been like if Madonna had picked different kissmates for her VMA performance? As it turns out, Pink was supposed to be there too.

"It was originally supposed to be different people involved," Pink told MTV News Europe, "but I didn't even know [what was going to happen during the performance]. The kiss wasn't even talked about."

Rumors had been rampant before the Video Music Awards that Madonna would be taking the stage with representatives of the younger pop generation, but talk had centered mostly on Britney Spears and Jennifer Lopez. Pink escaped the rumor mill because she backed out before rehearsals began.

"I had one vacation all year," Pink explained, "and I was not leaving my vacation to go into a rehearsal studio, which sounds ridiculous, but you know, I just said, 'I can't be at those rehearsals, but I can learn it for that day, and I'm sure that I'll be more than capable of doing it.' "

That proposal, however, wasn't acceptable, and Pink was taken out of the running. But since she was only apprised of the singing aspect of the performance, not the kissing, it begs the question: Had scheduling not been a problem, would she have been willing to make out with Madonna? She says yes and insists she's amused that the same-sex liplock generated so many headlines.

"I'm very surprised that kisses, or girl-on-girl, or guy-on-guy, is that interesting still, to this day," she laughed.

The Guardian: The other Madonna book.

"The Guardian" has published an ironic story about Madonna by Catherine Bennett. It's called "The English pose: a story about Madonna".

Once there was a country called England. The people who lived there thought they were terribly clever. In fact they were dreadfully old-fashioned and dreary. And if you don't believe me, just take a look around. Boy, were they stupid.

But the English people were lucky because one day a lovely young woman called Madonna came from far away to live among them. She was beautiful and kind with long golden hair and a singing voice like an angel, and all the ugly English people should have admired her a lot. But do you know what? They didn't! Can you believe that?

Poor, sad Madonna did everything she possibly could to make friends. She bought a mansion in the country and learned how to shoot. She made herself drink their disgusting beer and wore a flat tweed cap even though it looked ridiculous. She even learned how to speak English. But no matter how many times she said "Corblimey, me old cock," it made no difference. Not a goddamn bit. Madonna was the loneliest girl in the world.

One freezing night, while all the English people were out getting drunk on bitter, Madonna's fairy godfather, Andrew, came by. Here is something you should know about him: He was also a literary agent. "What's wrong?", said Andrew. "I'll never fit in here," sobbed Madonna. "Why can't they see how fabulous I really am?"

"Why don't you just show them in a book?" said Andrew.

"Oh brilliant," said Madonna. "Do you have any idea what having two nippers has done to my Bristol Cities?" Her tears continued to flow, quite soaking a supposedly impermeable pair of Swaine & Adeney shooting gaiters.

"Never fear!" cried Fairy Andrew. "On this occasion clothing will be provided. For a modest financial consideration." He waved his wand: "You shall write a bestseller!"

There was a flash and all of a sudden Madonna found that she was sitting in a beautiful garden surrounded by piles of books with her name on the cover. Why, when Fairy Andrew held up a mirror, she found she even looked like an author! Her incredible hair was swept up into an intellectual chignon; she wore spectacles and a prim, faux-vintage shift, just like Enid Potter or Beatrix Blyton might have done in the olden days. "But," Madonna whispered to Andrew, "but... I don't even like books."

"Trust me," responded that kind fairy. "They'll never guess."

Sure enough, when Madonna looked around, she found she was surrounded by smiling English people, all talking about her. And this is what they said:

"That Madonna is really something."

"What a beautiful woman."

"She looks even better in glasses."

You're not going to believe this, but for English people some of her new admirers were really quite famous. Nigella wanted to know who had made the cucumber sandwiches. Someone called Martin Amis came up and asked what kind of pen she used. Madonna sat him on her knee and was just about to explain everything when Fairy Andrew announced that Madonna was a very shy person and allergic to people over six. Listen: isn't that kind of service worth 10% of anyone's money?

Before anyone could ask another question Madonna ran out of the garden and down the stairs to the street. As she did so, her chignon tumbled down, her glasses fell off, and one of her precious floral-printed shoes dropped into the gutter.

Yeah, yeah, I know what you're thinking: how could she be sure it hadn't all been a dream? I'll tell you one thing: it wasn't because some thieving prince picked up the shoe. Madonna had already sent it back to Prada for a refund.

Still wanna know how? Got £12.99? Then go find your own copy of Madonna's book. I didn't make this up.

The Guardian: A charming story spoilt in the telling.
Michael Rosen, poet and writer of children's books such as We're Going on a Bear Hunt and Little Rabbit Foo-Foo

"The story itself is a simple moralistic story, which is slightly out of fashion these days. But Madonna has chosen to convey it in a very personalised way, talking directly to the children and telling them not to interrupt - a bit Roald Dahl-like. We are used to more irony and questioning in children's writing. Usually, publishers like to hide the moral aspect: the story "says" not "tells". The storyline in Madonna's book is very moralistic, the narrator tells you exactly what the lesson is. But the texture of the telling is fun.

However, the story's charm is completely undermined by the odious pictures. They are like mid-60s advertising copy cut-outs on "skinny chic" legs. They are repulsive and faintly unpleasant with big doe eyes and bowlips which never change.

Their form is both passive and sexual, it is Disney meets Twiggy, the waif. It all makes you feel slightly unpleasant.

Children learn how to read expressions from illustrations, but not from these illustrations. They convey no feeling: they are clothes pegs. They resemble a drawing of Twiggy to sell coats.

They show a very strange passivity which is not present in the telling of the story.

The book will sell trillions, one: because it is by Madonna and two: because the passive but sexual is seductive to children, even more so today.

It will appeal to sexualised seven-year-olds because it's as if [in the illustrations] they are being presented with the Cheeky Girls but they cannot speak."

Francesca Simon, children's author, creator of the Horrid Henry stories

I would have to say: "Mama, don't preach." The hectoring authorial voice keeps butting in - it is awkward, clumsy and preachy. You have to watch that in children's writing, you have to be charming. A good children's book has to leave space for the audience. There is a moral to discuss here, but so is there in the Bible stories. The English Roses has no characters, no story and there is no tension, which is a problem.

I can't imagine anyone reading this book more than once. I can't imagine any seven-year-old girl saying please read it to me again - everything is in the one reading.

The book is about girls who are bullies without realising they are, the casual cruelty of children and the shunning of other children. This is a subtle and valuable thing to explore, but I wish Madonna had written it better. I loved the last line, "I didn't make this up". But the four English Roses have no personality. The fifth girl is only defined by the fact that she is beautiful and like Cinderella.

It is not clear whom this book is aimed at. I am puzzled as to the use of the picture-book format when the illustrations look like 11- to 14-year-old teen models. They should be half the size and twice the width. The pictures are inappropriate.

On Amazon.com, the people who have bought this book have also bought David Beckham's autobiography, Trinny and Susannah's What Not to Wear and the Madonna encyclopedia. That says it all: this is a book aimed at adults. Just because you put a fairy in a story, it doesn't mean it's a book for children.

The Guardian: Pupils hear Madonna's morality tale.
In the reading corner at Islington's Hugh Myddleton primary school, year four stopped maths to hear a new book. The eight- and nine-year-olds were not sure if they would have been that excited about a new book if it had not been written by the pop icon Madonna.

"Didn't she kiss a girl, or was that Britney Spears?" wondered one boy, who knew her "from the newspapers".

The English Roses, published yesterday, had the biggest simultaneous world launch in publishing history in 30 languages and 100 countries. A story of four well-dressed and unwittingly bitchy girls, it purports to be a morality tale drawn from the Kabbalah, the school of Jewish mysticism which Madonna has studied for some years. When the four girls realise their beautiful, but ostracised, classmate has no mother and does all the cleaning at home, they relent and let her come on their picnics.

After the pop, the Hollywood films, the nudity and the foray on to the West End stage, Madonna, 45, aims to resuscitate her serious side with five children's morality tales by 2004. But like her London theatre debut, the book has had a mixed reception. First impressions have ranged from "a fabulous, affirmative ending" to suggestions that with "no characterisation, no story and a flat tone" it would never have been published if not for the name on the spine.

In year four, at least 10 pupils said their mothers had Madonna's albums. But Madonna was best known to the children "for those new adverts for trousers for the Gap". The global branding message aimed at the pop star's target audience of six- to nine-year-olds had clearly been achieved. The clothing chain, Gap, is stocking Madonna's book as part of its advertising deal.

Whether children can afford to buy the English Roses is a different issue. "Twelve pounds, ninety-nine pence? No way, it's more than Harry Potter," said Wasif. "Can I get it out of the library?"

It struck a painful chord with the children who had been bullied.

"It teaches you that instead of leaving people out because you are different to them, you should let them join in," said Alexis. Amy, who identified with the bullied Binah, said: "It teaches you should be nice to people who are new to your school." "It makes me feel like I did when it happened to me," said Jago. "It teaches you bullying is wrong." She admitted that other stories, poems and school lessons taught her bullying was wrong. Madonna's book, which was "like Cinderella", was not new.

But the pop star's finger-wagging narratorial style sometimes hit a blank wall. "Now stop interrupting me," says the narrator as if from nowhere. Year four, who had sat silent, patiently listening, looked faintly startled.

The bright, supermodel-style illustrations by the fashion artist Jeffrey Fulvimari were well received. "That's Barbie" and "that's Kylie" came the cries from the floor. "It looks like it was done on the internet," said Wasif, impressed.

Meanwhile Madonna appeared at the book's Paris launch last night, recovering from her announcement that she did not know who Enid Blyton was. Asked in London if she aimed to be the next Blyton, the American-born star looked blank and asked: "Who is that? Is she good?"

Toronto Star: Mixed reviews for author Madonna.
Twelve years after Madonna gave adults the raunchy picture-book "Sex", she has turned to a younger audience.

Her children's book, The English Roses, enjoyed a wide release yesterday, appearing in 100 countries and in 30 languages — including Estonian and Faroese.

The story, illustrated by American fashion artist Jeffrey Fulvimari, tells the tale of a talented and attractive young schoolgirl who is shunned by four other girls out of jealousy.

Madonna, who is 45, has said she conceived The English Roses as the first of five children's books derived from the Kabbalah, the system of Jewish mystical thought which she has been studying for seven years.

Her daughter Lourdes, who is 7, was the inspiration for the heroine, she told The Times Magazine in London.

In Lourdes' London school "children can be quite mean and ostracize her because I'm her mother," Madonna said. "Everyone thinks, `She's got everything so we won't pay attention to her.'"

In Madonna's book, the girls being mean, who are remarkably thin, well dressed and delicately featured themselves, eventually discover their nemesis is not so bad and invite her into their clique.

As of 4:30 p.m. yesterday, Amazon.com listed The English Roses (retailing in Canada for $28) at No. 6 on its U.S. bestseller list. Canadian buyers appeared to be less enthralled as Amazon.ca ranked the book at No. 82.

Jill Lawless, a London-based Associated Press book reviewer, said although the volume isn't likely to be a vocabulary-builder, it "trots along pleasantly enough."

"Madonna's authorial tone is matter-of-fact and agreeable, if a tad flat," she added.

As of noon yesterday, Eleanor LaFave of Mabel's Fables bookstore in Toronto said she'd received no calls enquiring about the book. She expected to see a greater response after Madonna appears on Oprah today.

Nancy Frater, owner of Booklore in Orangeville, also expects the book to find an audience.

"I think it's going to sell well, but we have to wait until the jury's in about the quality of the book," she said. "Some people could look at it and say `Oh it's just another celebrity writing a book.' But we shouldn't short-change it."

Adel Khalaf, who describes herself as a Madonna freak, said she heard about the book early yesterday, and scrambled to find a copy. She found one at the Eaton Centre Indigo."I've already read part of the book and it seems really good — the illustrations are great," she said. "I want to see what her writing is like in terms of relating to her kids."

Some buyers of the book have already posted reviews on Amazon.com. Madonna fan Benjamin Davis of London gave the book five stars while U.S. poster Michael E. Walker gave it only one, saying "Please do not purchase this. It is totally unacceptable. This is just her way of cashing in on motherhood."

USA Today: Madonna's children's book rocks the world market.
Children's books and Madonna. Hard to envision a less likely pairing. Yet today, 1 million copies of her The English Roses will be released in more than 100 countries and 30 languages, the largest simultaneous global release of a book.

Aimed at the 6-and-up crowd, the 48-page, $19.95 book is illustrated by American fashion illustrator Jeffrey Fulvimari. Distributed in the USA by Viking/Penguin, it is published by Callaway Editions.

The book explores feelings of envy among a group of 11-year-old girls in contemporary England, and there are autobiographical elements to the theme. The 45-year-old pop icon, whose mother died when she was young, told London's Times Saturday that as a child she ''felt very awkward and out of place in school. Not popular, not attractive, not special in any way, and I was longing for love and approval from someone.''

The book also draws on Madonna's study of the mystical Jewish Kabbalah teachings. Proceeds will go to the Los Angeles-based Spirituality for Kids Foundation.

AOL Time Warner Book Group CEO Laurence Kirshbaum is one of the few outsiders who has seen The English Roses, the first in a completed five-book series. (The second, Mr. Peabody's Apples, arrives Nov. 10.) Kirshbaum was the U.S. publisher of Madonna's 1992 controversial photo essay, Sex. Featuring Madonna in the buff before she had children Lourdes, 6, and Rocco, 3, or married Brit director Guy Ritchie, the book sold close to 2 million copies worldwide. Roses displays Madonna's ''consistent ability to reinvent herself,'' Kirshbaum says, citing her transformation from sex kitten to maternal oracle.

A celebrity's name on the cover, however, is no guarantee of success. Jamie Lee Curtis, John Lithgow, Bill Cosby and Will Smith have made their mark in children's publishing, but other celebrities have not. Recent books by Katie Couric and Lynne Cheney did not do as well. American Library Association spokeswoman Barbara Genco suspects the primary market for Madonna's book will be avid adult fans. No doubt they will be tuning in Tuesday when Madonna is scheduled to appear on The Oprah Winfrey Show.

''Publishers encourage (celebrities) to write children's books,'' says Roger Sutton, the editor of The Horn Book, which reviews children's literature. ''What message are you sending? 'You can't write a real book, so you can write a children's book?' ''

Sutton says creating a good children's book is difficult because ''you have to do a lot more with a lot fewer words.''

Smelling media manipulation, Sutton asks, ''Swapping spit with Britney Spears on MTV, what was that all about?'' But he doubts there will be much controversy about Roses. ''She's just not that big a deal anymore.''
