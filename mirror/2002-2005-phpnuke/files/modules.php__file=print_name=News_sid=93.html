SWEPT AWAY - an interview with Guy Ritchie.
Date: Thursday, June 12 @ W. Europe Daylight Time
Topic: Interviews

In this long, passionate interview (by Michael Fleming; Movieline magazine) Mr Ritchie talks about his love and devotion to Madonna, he explains why he chose to make the movie and how Madonna is on the set. Guy Ritchie talks about critics cruelty and his wife's heart and soul for her work.

Being quite long, the interview will be published in three parts. Here you can enjoy the first one.

MF: When you married Madonna, people speculated you’d put her in a movie right away. You denied it, but ended up doing exactly that. Why the change of heart?

GR: It was timing as much as anything. I was watching SWEPT AWAY on TV and the wife and I happened to be sitting on the bed together. I wasn’t paying attention to it. It seemed a bit camp to me. But then about 40 minutes into it – I was cutting my toenails by then or something – I suddenly got it and I loved it. By the end of the film, I said that someone should remake it. Madonna happened to be in proximity, and she was cheap and she was available. One thing led to another and three months later we were filming it. It was all pretty quick.

MF: You’re remaking what some consider a classic, featuring one of the most famous women in the world, who happens to be your wife. Did you ever worry what critics would say or did you just think: “This is a great idea, I’ll just go with it”?

GR: A bit of both. I’d like to say I don’t care what critics think, but they insidiously manage to work their way into your psyche. I‘m one of the most ill-informed persons you’ll ever come across, by virtue of the position we happen to be in. We don’t read the newspapers because we’re just fed up  with reading nasty things. Of course people write nice things, but I think, fuck it, none of it is supposed to affect how you think anyway. Swept Away is what it is. It is not an art film like the first one. It is more accessible, even if by virtue of the fact they speak English. The first was very good and I hope we’ve done a good job re-establishing its identity, even if the two aren’t really related.

MF: Did it take you a long time to get used to the press attention that comes with being married to Madonna?

GR: Yeah, and I’m not sure that I have yet. I’m aware of it and unaware of it. Once you demystify things…it’s the same as in film. Demystify what a director does, and it’s not a mystery anymore. When you’ve got a famous wife, when she’s your wife, it’s not a mystery anymore. We maintain pretty good privacy on the whole.

MF: Getting back to Swept Away, how is it different from the original?

GR: The first one was political and appropriate to the time. This is more about ecological issues, which seems to be a political agenda we all felt strongly about. In the original, the lady was a rich bird from Milan, with a rich husband from Milan. They were obviously Catholic. He fisherman was just a poor communist fisherman. Mine is about a rich American drug conglomerate governor who has brought his wife on the make to exploit nature. The fisherman is a fisherman, after all, and his livelihood is threatened by overfishing, chemicals, greed. That is our template.

MF: This is the first film you’ve made in which there is romance.

GR: Yeah, but I hope not in the traditional sense. I never fancy making a film where the woman is redundant and gorgeous. I wanted to make her tough. So it’s not a traditional love story. I mean, she takes a thrashing. She does take a bit of a kicking, which should make some people rather uneasy. But the film is essentially about getting what you need, not what you want. That can lead to some politically incorrect actions.

MF: The original was pretty sexy – those two roll around on the beach together. Does yours have that?

GR: It does. But I just get so bored of romances being predictable. I like the idea that this was so edgy. You almost couldn’t get away with wiping out the entire population of Somalia because they are the enemy, but you cannot slap a woman.. There is something interesting about that, not that I’m advocating slapping a woman. But it is a much hotter potato dealing with a woman who needs a slap.

MF: This is a complex character for Madonna to play because she goes from being a woman in control to being controlled. How do you coax that kind of performance out of her?

GR: She knew where we were going and I was aware of the pitfalls that someone like her could fall into, where with any other actress they wouldn’t give a shit. Because it is her, the critics are going to be tougher. I was very aware of that.
