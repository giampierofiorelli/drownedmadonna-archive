Swept Away

The English Roses

Ayckbourn attacked West End, celebrities and Madonna.
Alan Ayckbourn said yesterday that he would not take another of his plays to London's West End unless he had complete control over the production.

The playwright - the second most performed in the English language after Shakespeare - attacked the West End theatre world and its obsession with giving celebrities and film actors such as Ewan McGregor and Madonna the lead roles in plays.

He said he was unlikely to allow one of his works to be performed in the West End again, unless it changes its attitude considerably.

The author of 64 plays spoke of his frustrations to a packed house at the Edinburgh International Book Festival, which is sponsored by "The Herald" and the "Sunday Herald."

Sir Alan, 64, the first living playwright to be knighted since Noel Coward, and whose plays such as "Absurd Person Singular" and the "Norman Conquests" are among his most famous works, said there was now "no room" for new, one-off original plays in the West End, which had become overly-reliant on big-name celebrities and musicals.

"It seems to me to have become a very hostile environment for one-off plays ... what has happened is that theatre there has become reliant on TV stars or film actors to promote plays," he said.

"Although there are some successes such as Al Pacino, there are others like Madonna, who shouldn't be on the stage: she couldn't be heard, she is simply not a stage actress.

"It seems to me that theatre, instead of having its own stars, is relying on external casting that just doesn't work, and I think people are beginning to realise that now.

"Even when Ewan McGregor was on at the West End, [in Little Malcolm and his struggle against the Eunuchs] people were left disappointed," added Sir Alan.

In recent years, celebrities such as Madonna, Matthew Perry, Gillian Anderson, Nicole Kidman and Woody Harrelson have appeared on London stages with mixed results.

While Kidman was hailed as "theatrical Viagra" for her role in "The Blue Room," Madonna and Perry received poor reviews.

Sir Alan - whose writing career starter in 1959 - admitted he had been left disappointed by his last experience of staging a play in London. His trilogy of plays, called "Damsels in Distress," at the Duchess Theatre, had glowing reviews from critics but its running order was altered for commercial reasons.

He said earlier this year that the trilogy's commercial London producers, including Andrew Lloyd Webber's "Really Useful Theatres," had "condemned two of the parts to the dustbin".

He had wanted the three parts to be played in repertory, alternating throughout the week.

Instead, a decision had been taken over his head to perform only the first part, "Role Play," on weekday nights, with the full trilogy shown only on Saturdays.

He said this meant the other parts, "Game Plan" and "Flat Spin," were seldom seen, and described it as a "sad epilogue" to the whole affair.

Sir Alan said that he would require complete control of any new production if his new play, "Sugar Daddies," was to be staged in London's theatreland.

"I will never take a play to the West End unless it's on my own terms," he said.

"I am not asking for the moon, I am just asking for it to be done properly.

"We have not got a Ralph Fiennes or a Hollywood star, we just have good actors. I would like people in the West End to see good actors, not just two 'megastars' and four people whom they have managed to coerce on to the stage with them."

There has been a stream of Hollywood stars on the stage in recent years.

Gillian Anderson, from the "X Files," appeared in "What the Night is For;" Glenn Close was in "A Streetcar Named Desire;" Madonna appeared in "Up for Grabs" in 2002; and "Gwyneth Paltrow" was in "Proof," at the Donmar Warehouse.

Perry received negative reviews for his performance in David Mamet's "Sexual Perversity in Chicago" at the Comedy Theatre, and there were also mixed reviews for Kyle MacLachlan and Woody Harrelson in John Kolvenbach's "On an Average Day," also at the Comedy Theatre.

However, not all of the transfers from screen to stage have been unsuccessful.

Kidman received her glowing reviews for "The Blue Room" at the Donmar Warehouse in 1998, while Kevin Spacey was acclaimed for his role in Eugene O'Neill's "The Iceman Cometh" at the Almeida Theatre in the same year.



Note: Source: The Herald.
