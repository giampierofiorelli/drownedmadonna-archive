Look
Go for baroque

The lavish styles of past centuries have been reinvented on the runways and in the wardrobes of stars such as Madonna.

by SYLVI CAPELACI, Special to The Free Press   2004-08-10 02:13:16  

Corsetry will be coveted next season. It's inevitable, especially since Esther --the Material Girl formerly known as Madonna -- keeps flashing hers in her current Re-Invention world tour.

The sexy nude and gold glittery number she wears to open the show -- continually photographed right side up, upside down and in profile with bosom overflowing -- makes headlines in every city in which she performs.

French designer Christian LaCroix was called upon to liberate Madonna from this typically binding and inhibiting garment, Women's Wear Daily has reported.

LaCroix, an expert in theatre performance costumes, reconfigured Madonna's corsetry by making the boning bendable and fabrics far-reaching to enable the diva to move freely on stage -- back arches and all. And it seems the pop icon is breaking a costume record (if there is such a thing).

Women's Wear Daily also noted she's taking 14 big-name designers, including Dolce & Gabbana, Jean-Paul Gaultier, Stella McCartney and Karl Lagerfeld for Chanel on her 14-city North American tour.

Style's pendulum always swings back to push forward. For fall, designers are blowing the dust off fashion's most historic silhouettes. Besides corsetry are hoop skirts, bustles and leg-of-mutton sleeves.

Pick any style -- baroque, rococo, Victorian, Edwardian -- and designers have gone petticoat over panier for costumey Old World clothing.

The couture muse of Christian Dior designer John Galliano was the rococo icon Marquise de Pompadour. Jean-Paul Gaultier's was the swashbuckling Scarlet Pimpernel and dashing musketeers. Miuccia Prada cruised and perused Victorian archives for her resort collection.

"Styles have been so tight, tailored and sucked in to the body for so long," says Royal Ontario Museum fashion curator and author Alexandra Palmer. The time was ripe for change.

"Softly draped with a space between the textile and the body is more discreet. It does evoke a sense of history . . . , capturing the fleeting," she says.

Trend watchers agree only select details from fashion's billowing and voluminous past -- not all -- will become huge hits on the streets. You won't have to dip to swaggering lengths to pouf up your fall wardrobe.

The goal is not to look like the Marquise de Pompadour or Marie Antoinette. A corset here or a swooshing cape there may be all you need.

Key moments in the femme fatale silhouette

17th-century baroque

Fashion was flamboyant and marked with elaborate decoration. "It was a time of incredibly lavish textiles, jewel tones and very long corsetry for women. Men wore jerkins (sleeveless tunics), high boots with heels, plumed hats and long wigs or long hair. It was a floppy look," says Palmer.

18th century

Men wore lace jabots and breeches like the men in Madonna's current Re-Invention tour. Women were carefully corseted and wore wide hip paniers (a basket-like framework) and bell-shaped hooped petticoats under their skirts.

During the French rococo period, fashion featured rich decoration, lace frills and excessively ornate embroidery. The Marquise de Pompadour and men who dressed like the elusive Scarlet Pimpernel were style icons. 1850-'60s Victorian v-shape

Corsetry was used to create tiny waists exploding into big bell skirts with hoop crinolines propping them up and out. We all watched Vivien Leigh getting laced into her corset in Gone With the Wind. "The look was an extremely defined v-shape from shoulder to waist," says Palmer.

1870s mid-Victorian silhouette

Soft rounded bustles replaced big hoop crinolines, shifting the focus and volume to the buttock.

1880s late-Victorian silhouette

"Women can now balance a tea cup on their bustle," says Palmer of "the new hard bustle." This silhouette featured a dramatic vertical line in the front, while the back jutted shelf-like with an almost 90-degree projection.

1900s Edwardian era

Welcome to the s-bend's glory days. On profile, the female form featured the chest pushed forward to contrast her rounded bottom thrust back. Women wore s-shaped, steel-boned corsetry to achieve this exaggerated shape.

1920-'30s Art Deco period

Flappers with flat-linear boyish figures were all the rage. The feminization of this slender, youthful androgynous frame was called "the garconne look." Women sported short bob haircuts and straight, loose sleeveless drop-waist dresses, institutionalized by Chanel.

1947 Dior's 'new look'

This revolutionary silhouette featured rounded shoulders, a shapely bustline, nipped-in waist, slightly padded curvaceous hips and full skirts reaching below the calves. Skirts were often accompanied by wide, flounced petticoats. "It was a look reminiscent of the 19th-century Victorian period," notes Palmer.

1950s P- and S-shapes

Skirt and sweater girls -- like Jane Russell -- played to the p-shape, while the voluptuous Marilyn Monroe defined the super s-curve.

1960s swinging London

Stick-thin model Twiggy was the hot bod on runways and in fashion magazines. But it was curvy Barbie (born in 1959) that little girls idolized. Andre Courreges pushed fashion into the future, while Audrey Hepburn and Jacqueline Kennedy brought grace and elegance to the decade.

1970s hedonism

Granola kept body fat to a minimum in this decade. So did escalating drug use and dancing the night away at Studio 54. Halston is the reigning king of New York night life.

1980s excel and excess

Fitness-obsessed Jane Fonda-types in Flashdance legwarmers strove to achieve athletic, hard bodies. Meanwhile, TV's Dynasty clan revitalized the broad-shouldered suit for day and glitzy gowns for evenings. By the late '80s, Christian LaCroix's pouff silhouette became the toast of Paris.

1990s thin is in

Runway models such as Kate Moss inspired the masses to mimic her rakish-thin androgynous silhouette. This skeletal body frame became the prerequisite for fashion's grunge movement, followed by that dark, drug-induced period known as heroin chic.

2000 voluptuous victory

Women get curvy again. Blame it on J.Lo. Stats are up on breast implants and gluteoplasty procedures. Butts and bustlines ride high on fashion runways with built-in bustles on dresses (Gaultier) and corset lacing (Dior, LaCroix). Madonna's Re-Invention tour sparks renewed interest in corsetry. Simultaneously, there is a major revival of period-inspired silhouettes on designers runways -- bringing fashion the full hooped circle.

PERIOD DETAILS TO WATCH FOR

- Wasp-waisted silhouettes

- Rear view: Peplums, small bustles or ruffle details draw attention to the buttocks.

- Corsetry: For daytime, evening or in the boudoir.

- Capes: Take the current poncho trend to long, flowing lengths. Capelets are another option.

- Thigh-high musketeer-inspired boots

- Ornate footwear that takes its cue from Louis Vuitton's fur-lined and ribbon-laced booties in the Edwardian carriage style.

- Deluxe details and flourishes in fur, ribbon, lace and opulent embroideries inspire the return to romance.

- Bold millinery for brave hearts: Think plumed cowboy hats, netted coquettish confections, broad-brimmed sombreros and royal crowns sitting askew on bouffant dos.
