Sarah Berhardt, the 19th Century Madonna
Documenting a diva
TRIBUNE-REVIEW

Imagine a larger-than-life star, a woman whose every public foray is documented. A woman who courts controversy, directly and indirectly, zestfully sparring with the press and her detractors. A woman whose outrageous behavior often overshadows her art.

Madonna? Yes, of course. But at the turn of the 20th century, the above was also true of Sarah Bernhardt, the French daughter of a courtesan who became a world-renowned actress.

Author Adam Braver has fictionalized a week in Bernhardt's life in "Divine Sarah."
"Divine Sarah" takes place in 1906. Bernhardt is 61, her best days behind her. She is waiting for her troupe to arrive in Venice, Calif., where she's scheduled to perform the role of Marguerite Gautier in "La Dame aux Camellias."

It should be the best time of her life, the living legend onstage before adoring throngs. Yet controversy dogs her; Bernhardt is in Venice because the League of Decency, via the auspices of the Archbishop of Los Angeles, has successfully barred her company from playing the City of Angels.

What were her crimes? She was indecent, perhaps a homosexual who wore men's clothing onstage and had the audacity to perform the role of Hamlet. Never mind that men frequently performed as women onstage.

"She got in a lot of trouble with what we would now call the religious right," Braver says. "They were always really waging this indecency battle against her -- and they were pretty vicious, if you read the accounts. She'd fight back with them. In the book, I mutate an incident where she gets up onstage and writes a check out to them for the publicity."

While "Divine Sarah" is fictional, Braver bases many of his ideas and themes on fact. In the book, Bernhardt struggles with the role of Marguerite Gautier, attempting to redefine the character. In reality, Bernhardt played Gautier in the Alexandre Dumas play well into her 70s, despite the fact that the role was written for a girl the age of 16 or 17.

Braver thinks that's a considerable difference from Bernhardt's modern-day counterpart. Both Madonna and Bernhardt used provocation as a means to draw attention to their work, and both became global figures.

But unlike the Material Girl -- especially in some of her recent movie roles -- Sarah Bernhardt rarely suffered bad reviews.

"The one major difference -- and I get in trouble when I say this with Madonna fans -- is Sarah Bernhardt's talent was never in question," Braver says. "Meaning nobody just saw her as a personality. She was recognized as the greatest actress of the times."
