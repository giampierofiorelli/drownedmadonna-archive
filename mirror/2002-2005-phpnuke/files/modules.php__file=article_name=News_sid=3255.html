Trying to spark controversy
What Still Shocks Is
Madonna's Showmanship
At 45, the Material Girl is still trying to spark controversy.

BY JIM FUSILLI
Wednesday, June 23, 2004 12:01 a.m. EDT

NEW YORK--The cracks are starting to show in the Madonna juggernaut. At 45, she finds herself a bit outside a mainstream pop scene littered with young women content to emulate her sex-driven approach. Her most recent recording, last year's "American Life" (Warner Bros.) sold poorly, at least by her standards. As a pop-music maker, she's at a career junction.

Despite having a thin voice that's distinctive only because of its familiarity, Madonna has made some recordings that are blissful examples of great, hook-laden pop. Since she still has her pick of the best songwriters and producers, you'd think her music could be her focus.

But controversy, rather than music, still defines her in the public consciousness, and now her capacity to shock is limited by the extremes to which she has already gone. This conundrum is on display on stage during performances of her current "Re-Invention Tour," which finds her music and showmanship as enjoyable as ever and her taste for controversy tired and dull.

Her latest hullabaloo is about whether she's sincerely interested in Judaism. She's joined the trendy, Los Angeles-based Kabbalah Centre that promotes mysticism and sells red string bracelets that are said to protect wearers against the evil eye; Madonna wears one on stage during the tour's shows. A faux controversy, this, even when she dons a "Kabbalists Do It Better" T-shirt: For one, since she's used sex, marriage, Christianity and American patriotism as promotional tools, why not Judaism? And as an American of Italian descent, Madonna already knows that you ward off the malocchio with a red pepper.

For her devoted fans, controversy is part of the package and little reinvention is necessary. Bearing smiles, her legions arrived last week at Madison Square Garden here and at Washington's MCI Center, ready to sing along, dance along, strike a pose, and have the time of their lives, even if the top ticket went for more than $300. I joined them at both venues, though her publicists denied me the usual journalists' complimentary tickets by saying they were limiting passes to those of us who've been with her a long time, "with her" being the operative phrase.

The reinvention, it turns out, is modest but effective. Arrangements have been cleverly updated, and the new textures are wonderful. Late in the show, while Madonna was offstage, a drum corps and bagpipe player took over, and soon Madonna and her troupe of singers and dancers reappeared in tight undershirts and flowing kilts to join in for a revamped, percussion-heavy "Get Into the Groove" that morphed into an exhilarating version of her first hit, "Holiday." The relentless whirring rhythms were built on a sound not unlike kettle drums. 

Madonna and the troupe performed several medleys that brought together old and newer material: "Express Yourself" followed the title track from "American Life"; and "Lament" from the "Evita" soundtrack succeeded another "American Life" tune, "Die Another Day." The former coupling, up-tempo and crisp, returned Madonna to her dance-club roots, while the latter showed her capability with grand ballads; though tailored for her limited range, the songs have an inherent drama that lent the 75-minute concert its appealing sense of dynamics.

Though she performed several songs with only her backing band, one of which was a boisterous reworking of "Burning Up," the "Re-Invention Tour" is about spectacle, and spectacular it was. Only the churlish would complain about the skateboarder, the acrobats swinging dangerously high above the stage, the tap dancer, the fire spinner and the inventive sets, many of which use videos perfectly married to the live action: the helicopter landing for the depiction of military battles for "American Life," for example; or the soul choir for the evening's best performance, "Like a Prayer." At various times, she evoked Kurt Weill and the Weimar Republic, and Paris in the 1930s; the dance number for "Hanky Panky" wouldn't have been out of place in Baz Luhrmann's "Moulin Rouge."

As for the controversy: there's a nun in a mini-skirt, and dancers dressed up as religious figures are attacked; a video showed children injured in military conflicts; following a precision rifle drill in which she's dressed in khaki and a beret, Madonna gives the finger to . . .well, it's not clear to whom. Hebrew symbols filled the video screen during two songs, though without explanation; some of us might've thought it said nothing more enlightening than "Kabbalists Do It Better."

This need for the spark of controversy ought to be beneath Madonna by now. Her brand of pop music is really good at times, and her show dazzled. And though she can seem distracted by the precision the choreography requires, Madonna worked so hard on stage that she ignited a crowd that's already burning with affection for her: Rarely have I seen such joy in audiences; they were positively giddy at times. Evenings this special don't require lame political statements or unfocused provocations. Clearly, music and genuine showmanship were the best parts of the event, and the only ones that mattered
