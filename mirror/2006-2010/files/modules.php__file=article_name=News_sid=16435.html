The Guardian: Madonna Fur Debate Is Illogical

I'd save the cow

December 8, 2006 04:44 PM

Madonna made herself the latest target of animal rights activists when she was recently photographed leaving a London restaurant wrapped in the skins of forty odd chinchillas. According to the Daily Star, she is now "living in terror". The tabloid dedicates a double page spread to the story with lots of alluring snaps of scantily clad bodies posing for PETA's "I'd rather go naked than wear fur" campaign. Trouble is, I can't help but feel that the public's getting a bit bored of anti-fur and anti-animal testing stories.

The current debate is illogical. Why is it front-page news when Madonna is photographed wearing rodents but fine when David Hasselhoff steps out in a new pair of leather trousers? Both products are made from dead animals that have been farmed and killed for the sake of fashion, but cows don't seem to matter and chinchillas do. Given the choice, I'd save the cow.

It is by no means essential to wear animal products, but I do believe it is sometimes necessary to experiment on them for the sake of other humans. A couple of weeks ago I watched BBC2's Monkeys, Rats and Me: Animal Testing documentary, which portrays both sides of the animal experimentation argument - those campaigning to end it, and those demonstrating the benefits it can bring. Watching a monkey confined in a small cage as it learns to touch icons on a computer screen is grim viewing, and it is made harder to swallow when you learn that the creature's future holds more of the same but with an electrode in its head. On the other hand, surely this is worth it when you see a boy formerly confined to a wheelchair stand up?

What interests me is not that humans can get so worked up over animals, but that there is no logic to their targets. If I wanted to protest, I'd target cases that resulted in the most pain for the least point.

One campaigner, filmed while harassing workers working on the new Oxford animal research facility construction site, shouted to the builders that they should go and get a job at McDonald's, presumably unaware that the average animal bred for human consumption has suffered a good deal more than an anaesthetised rat on an operating table.

Chickens are kept in dreadful conditions (hocks burned by their own urine, beaks filed down to prevent pecking) to be priced at a couple of pounds by a large supermarket. Pigs born to mothers unable to stand in farrowing crates end up on your plate as affordable bacon and sausages. Cows suffer mastitis just to supply gallon upon gallon of cheap milk. If people cared more about where their food came from and paid an honest price for it, this real and unnecessary suffering could be avoided.

Not only do we eat animals that have led unhappy lives, but other examples of harming animals abound in our culture. ITV employees work for a company that broadcasts the consumption of live crickets and dissected kangaroos at prime time. The manufacturers of commercially used pesticides wipe out plagues of green fly and sending all manner of poison up the food chain. And as for the dog owners who stuff their pets full of chocolate until they can barely get off the couch - well, I doubt they sleep easy at night.

The unfortunate Australian wildlife that made it on to I'm a Celebrity doesn't particularly bother me, but the food industry and the fat dogs do. It is just unfortunate that A-listers in warm coats sell newspapers and disabled chickens don't.

Source: Katherine Le Ruez, The Guardian
