Why Madonna and I are doing the right thing

Why Madonna and I are doing the right thing

I've never had much in common with Madonna.

Until recently.

Last month, the pop diva made headlines by beginning adoption proceedings for a 13-month-old baby boy from Malawi.

When I first heard the news, I thought, great, maybe this will focus American attention on Malawi, a beautiful but desperately poor country where I lived and worked for three years as a Peace Corps volunteer.

Malawi, just south of Tanzania, has been in the news before. About two years ago, it experienced a horrific drought and was on the edge of famine. People starved.

Remember?

Neither does anyone else.

That tragedy merited a brief notation in American newspapers, including the Savannah Morning News.

Madonna will make more of an impression.

I also thought Madonna's actions might open Malawi up for foreign adoption. When my husband and I decided about six years ago that we wanted to adopt internationally, Malawi was the first country we considered.

Much like Madonna, who started a nonprofit called Raising Malawi for the nation's AIDS orphans, we already had an affinity for the country. We hadn't given millions of dollars, but we had volunteered what was then about a fifth of our working lives. We knew the culture. We had friends there. It made sense.

But we quickly learned that Malawi had no structure in place for international adoption. An inquiry to an American acquaintance who ran an AIDS orphan assistance program was discouraging.

"We don't want to be seen as taking their children away," he said.

Wanting to be parents, rather than pioneers, we looked elsewhere, eventually adopting quite happily from Vietnam.

I'm glad Madonna was willing to blaze a trail. Plenty of other people, though, aren't happy. Even other adoptive parents have accused her of skirting the rules.

I haven't seen evidence that that's true. Somebody has to be the first to adopt internationally anywhere. Harry and Bertha Holt needed an act of Congress to adopt from Korea in 1955, but now they're hailed as adoption heroes.

The backlash against Madonna took me by surprise. But it seems celebrity adoptions serve as a crucible, intensifying the feelings people have about international and transracial adoption.

And lots of those feelings are negative.

Both my sister and a co-worker said Madonna "bought a baby." They used that word. Bought.

"A baby is the hot personal accessory in Hollywood," I overheard the co-worker say. "That's why Madonna had to go out and buy her one from Africa."

Do they think I bought my son?

Yes, it cost thousands of dollars to adopt him. It also costs thousands of dollars to have a cesarean section, but it's never once occurred to me that my co-worker bought her son.

Madonna's also been criticized for not adopting a "true orphan." The argument goes like this: Because little David Banda's father is still living, Madonna should have supported Yohane Banda and allowed him to raise his son himself.

It's the case in almost every international adoption, and in many domestic ones, that birth parents are poorer than the adoptive ones. Yes, the gap isn't usually as wide as that between the Material Girl and a Third World subsistence farmer. But it's only a matter of degree. The wealth that most of us have is astonishing compared to the average Malawian, who lives without running water or electricity.

My son's birthmother is living. She decided not to raise him, likely the most difficult decision a mother could ever make.

If you think Madonna should be sending monthly checks to Yohane Banda, then there are tens of thousands of us average Americans who should be doing the same rather than raising our adopted children.

We didn't adopt to become charitable organizations. We adopted to become parents.

Madonna already has a charitable organization. Isn't is just possible she adopted David to mother him, complete with all the joy and heartache that's likely to come with it for the rest of her life?

A final concern: Madonna picked David after being introduced to other children in other orphanages. So what? We're in the process of adopting a second child, this one a little boy I first saw on an Internet photolisting of waiting children. I relish the idea of telling him some day that I chose him.

After all, love is a decision.

Remember "I do"?

I don't pretend to know what's in Madonna's heart. But she's made the same decision I did to love a child.

I wish her and little David all the best.

As they say in Malawi whenever friends or family part, "Tsalani bwino."

Stay well.

Source: Mary Landers,  Savannah Morning News
