BREAKING NEWS: Delaware judge: Warner Music, Maverick can pursue lawsuits in LA

Associated Press

LOS ANGELES - A judge on Friday cleared the way for Madonna's Maverick Records label to proceed with a lawsuit filed here against Warner Music Group, temporarily staying a separate case brought earlier in Delaware by the record company against its joint venture partner.

Delaware Court of Chancery Vice Chancellor Leo E. Strine Jr., ruled that the legal disputes could be tried in Los Angeles, on condition that the case go to trial no later than early November, representatives of both companies said Friday.

Strine stipulated both sides had until May 18 to secure a trial date from the Los Angeles Superior Court system within that time frame, or he would proceed with the case in Delaware.

"The judge basically went along with the Maverick position that it should be tried in California," attorney Bertram Fields, who is representing the pop singer and her partners at Maverick Records, said Friday after the court hearing.
"That's where the witnesses are, that's where the parties are, but only if it can be done promptly."

In a statement Friday, Warner Music officials said they were pleased with Strine's emphasis on expediting the case.

"Regardless of where the trial ultimately is held, we are confident the court will agree that Maverick's claims against the company are baseless and that WMG has met all of its financial and legal obligations to the joint venture."

Warner Music, home to Madonna since she released her 1983 self-titled debut, launched the Maverick label as a joint venture with the singer in 1992. Madonna and two partners own a combined 60 percent stake in the label, whose artist roster includes Alanis Morissette and Michelle Branch. Warner owns the remaining 40 percent.

Madonna and her partners contend that Warner fell short of its commitment to help cover operating expenses for the label. The current contract between Maverick and Warner is set to expire at the end of this year, and both sides have been trying to negotiate a settlement to the dispute for months.

In March, Warner Music sued Maverick, Madonna and her label partners. The lawsuit, filed in Delaware, asked the court to affirm that Maverick's claims are baseless. The company also asserted that Maverick's management had racked up on its own some $66 million in losses since 1999.

The next day, Maverick countersued in Los Angeles, accusing Warner Music and Time Warner of breach of contract. The label alleged that the record company and its former parent engaged in improper accounting and management that cost the singer and her partners millions of dollars.

Maverick claims Warner violated the terms of their venture agreement by failing to pay for "guaranteed" services like radio promotion, marketing and sales meant to support Maverick artists.

The lawsuit asks the court to declare that the three partners have the right to end the joint partnership agreement and do business with other companies. Madonna and her partners also seek unspecified compensatory and punitive damages.

Warner Music has called Maverick's claims "baseless, unsubstantiated and without merit."

Read It First Here: Out Of Your Mouth Face The

By now you’ve probably heard the rock cover of Madonna’s "Music" on your local radio station or have seen the video on MuchMusic and wondered who the hell the band is.

No, it’s not Orgy — the band in question are Calgary’s Out Of Your Mouth and the cover is the first single off their soon to be released debut, Draghdad.

Like so many other covers, the song immediately caught the attention of a lot of listeners. Lead singer Jason Darr is pleased with the response people have had with it considering that the song originally wasn’t even supposed to be recorded.

"It’s one of those songs that always found it’s way into our set," says Darr. "A lot of people commented on how great it was and said, ‘You guys should record that and put that on your record.’ So we suggested it to the record company, we played it for them and they absolutely flipped out. So that’s where the song came from."

And it’s a good thing they did record it. Who knows what would’ve happened if the band had try to launch their career with an original tune? Whatever the outcome, it probably wouldn’t top the attention that this once-unknown band from Calgary are getting now.

"It had actually ended up getting released a little earlier then we had planned," Darr says. "When our people were doing a meeting with MuchMusic, Much just flipped out and said, ‘We’ve got to start playing this now!’ So that started the ball rolling early."

And what does the Material Girl think about the raucous rendition of her pop hit?

"I don’t think she cares," Darr admits. "There’s probably so many other people covering her tunes, so it’s just one more band covering a Madonna song."

He’s right, Madge probably doesn’t care. But thanks to her, Out Of Your Mouth have started off with a bang. With the May 18 release of Draghdad still pending, the boys are looking to set the Canadian rock scene on fire.

Speaking of fire, I ask Darr if he was proud of his Calgary Flames and their play in the fist round of the NHL playoffs against the Vancouver Canucks.

"The Toronto Maple Leafs have been doing their warm-up skate to our tune," Darr said. "I think I’m just going to say that any team that warms up to my tune has got my support. The Flames are doing good now and I hope that they do well, but we’re getting a ton of support from Toronto with MuchMusic and the Edge [radio station] and now the Leafs, it’s really cool. So for that I got to give them some support back."

Unfortunately, Darr then adds, "I couldn’t tell you the name of one player on the team, but nonetheless I know they’re warming up to 'Music,' so what the hell."

And with that comment, a significant number of Calgary and Toronto hockey fans have just lost any interest they had in the young upstarts known as Out Of Your Mouth. And they were doing so well, too.

New York Post on Madonna vs Warner

PageSIX:

April 28, 2004 -- MADONNA was close to settling with Warner Music Group when the company's new owner, Edgar Bronfman Jr., ended the negotiations and sued her, Maverick's lawyer says. The Material Mom and Warner jointly own Maverick and are trying to dissolve the partnership; Bronfman claimed Maverick had lost $64 million since 1999. Maverick attorney Bert Fields told PAGE SIX: "First, Warner Music stabbed Madonna in the back by filing this suit in Delaware just as we were on the verge of settling this. Then, they turn their p.r. department loose to smear her." Fields said Warner honchos used "creative accounting" to come up with losses at Maverick, whose artist roster included Alanis Morissette and Michelle Branch. "Maverick has earned a handsome profit actually, $100 million over the course of 10 years," Fields says. He's also annoyed the case was filed in Delaware, necessitating thousands of frequent-flier miles for Maverick's small staff and witnesses in L.A. Fields added that Warner Music might have trouble signing new artists "the way they have treated Madonna. They sued her and then they smeared her. That's no way to run a business." Warner Music had no comment.

Alanis Morrisette's brand-new album.
Recipients of an advance copy of a CD by Arthur Moore, sent out by Madonna's Maverick label, probably thought the disc was just another example of the labels many failures (Ebo, Home Town Hero, Lillix etc.). In short: Just about anyone on Maverick not named Alanis Morrisette. But this poor match of the first name of a character from a Liza Minelli film and the last name of the actor who played him is really a code name for Ms. Morissette, and the Arthur Moore disc is in fact a copy of her much anticipated new release, So Called Chaos, due out May 18. The deceptive naming is just one feature of the most extreme—and silly—effort yet by a label to keep new music from being shared by fans on the Internet before its official release. Each review copy of So Called Chaos has a coded watermark with the name of the person who received the disc written silently into the digital code, so leaks can be traced. Further, on the disc is a warning that secret copy-protection software may introduce a "playability issue." In short, the disc is so well protected that it will not play in DVD players, most computers, some portable CD players, automobile CD players and most standard equipment. Critics unable to play the official version, meanwhile, can—no surprise—find the songs from So Called Chaos already beginning to appear on the major peer-to-peer file-sharing networks.

Maverick uses file-sharing data to boost sales

from miami.com:

By Dawn C. Chmielewski

Mercury News

It was one of those sunglasses-required summer days in Los Angeles when Eric Garland, a leading expert on music downloading, arrived for his meeting with a senior media company executive. Rather than talking in the company's air-conditioned offices, the executive led Garland and his partner through a fetid back alley to a secluded courtyard

Only then did the executive ask his question: Which songs, exactly, are the millions of Napster users illegally downloading? ``I just thought, this is crazy,'' recalled Garland, who had to prop his laptop on a dumpster to give his presentation.

Record-label executives discreetly use Garland's research firm, BigChampagne, and other services to track which songs are traded online and help pick which new singles to release. They increasingly use such file-sharing data to persuade radio stations and MTV to give new songs a spin or boost airplay for those that are popular with downloaders.

Some labels even monitor what people do with their music after they download it to better structure deals with licensed downloading services. The ultimate goal is what it always has been in the record business: Sell more music.

``I know of a case where an artist had obviously gone with the wrong single, and everyone loved this other song they had on their record,'' said Guy Oseary, Madonna's business partner and head of her label, Maverick Records. ``In the world of what we do, it's always good to have real information from real fans.''

Maverick used BigChampagne's 100-city breakdown of popularly downloaded songs to persuade radio stations to start playing a new band, Story of the Year, during prime daytime listening hours instead of at night.

The online data revealed that despite Story of the Year's lunar rotation, its single ``Until the Day I Die'' ranked among the top 20 most popular downloads, alongside tracks from Blink-182, Audioslave and Hoobastank that received significantly more airplay. And when the band performed in a city, ``we didn't necessarily see the phones blowing up at radio, but we saw download requests for the song skyrocket as they went through,'' said Jeremy Welt, Maverick's head of new media.

Armed with this data, Maverick fought for more airtime at radio, which translated into more CD sales. Story of the Year's album, ``Page Avenue,'' just went gold, selling more than half a million copies.

``I definitely don't like to spin it that piracy is OK because we get to look at the data. It's too bad that people are stealing so much music,'' said Welt. ``That said, we would be very foolish if we didn't look and pay attention to what's going on.''
