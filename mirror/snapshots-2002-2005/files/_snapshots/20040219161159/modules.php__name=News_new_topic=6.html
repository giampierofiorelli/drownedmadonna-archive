A signed book by Madonna in auction.
The Prince of Wales is helping Camilla Parker Bowles’s son to launch his latest business venture.

Tom Parker Bowles and his cousin Ben Elliot are behind Quintessentially Magazine, a new lifestyle publication aimed at the well-heeled.

It is being launched on Tuesday with a celebrity auction and the most prized lot is one of Charles’s own art works.

He has donated a signed lithograph for the auction, which will be the highlight of a star-studded party at Victoria House in London’s Bloomsbury Square.

Other lots include a magnum of champagne signed by Cold Mountain co-stars Jude Law and Nicole Kidman; a signed book by Madonna; a limited edition book on Diana, Princess of Wales; signed cricket bats from Imran Khan, Ian Botham and David Gower; and designer goodies from Jimmy Choo, Louis Vuitton and Burberry.

Two tickets to a Playboy event anywhere in the world are also up for grabs.

All money raised from the auction will go to the Prince’s Trust.

Royals on the guest list include Lord Freddie Windsor and Prince and Princess Michael of Kent.

Other invitees are Jeffrey Archer, Sophie Anderton, Claire Sweeney and Lord Brocket.

The magazine is the latest offshoot of Quintessentially, a members-only “bespoke concierge service” founded by Elliot in 2000.

The first edition of the quarterly magazine will go on sale later this month and contributors will include model-turned-writer Sophie Dahl.

Rumours: Has Madonna had a facelift?

Here's a disgusting article published by the Daily Mail:

By now, of course, we should have wearied of Madonna's endless capacity for re-invention.

What a cliché the remaking of her image has become as, year after year, she has struggled to keep the public intrigued.

But for all that there was something shockingly different about her appearance at the Grammy awards on Sunday night.

This time, it wasn't her usual gimmicky splash of fancy dress (a flashed breast, a pretend punk ensemble).

Nor was it as vulgar as the 'lesbian' show which she put on with Britney Spears at the MTV awards last year.

Instead, Madonna appeared to be showing off (whisper it) a new face.

Well that, at least, is what music industry gossips have insisted since Christmas, when, it is said, she visited a plastic surgeon in Los Angeles and had a 'very subtle and high quality' facelift.

Her appearance at the Grammys was the first chance for everyone outside her immediate circle to judge if the stories could possibly be true.

At 45, many of the well-heeled women in LA are on their third or fourth procedures, so there would be nothing outlandish, in Hollywood terms, about Madonna getting her face tweaked.

And talk of her using Botox injections has been widespread for some years now.

Sources in New York even claim to have spotted Madonna in town carrying a bag marked The Brandt Clinic, famed for its Botoxing.

Last year, it emerged that she'd been having non-surgical beauty serums blasted into her skin at a small salon in Hampstead, North London, for £350 a time.

Already had beauty serums blasted into her skin

She is also a fan of Linda Meredith's Crystal Clear Oxygen Facials and Dr Hauschka creams.

But a surgical facelift? Would Madge have gone that far to keep up with today's divas?

Surely she cannot hope to catch glorious, golden Beyonce, only 22, whose single Crazy In Love outsold Madonna's effort, Hollywood, last year by a ratio of two to one, and who walked away on Sunday with five awards, the acknowledged queen of this year's event.

Certainly, it appeared that Madonna had conceded defeat to the new generation of upstarts when she launched herself as a children's author last summer, all hornrimmed glasses and demure frock.

And with a flop album (American Life), two flop singles (American Life, Hollywood), a flop film (Swept Away) and a particularly embarrassing ad campaign under her belt (Gap), it appeared she was finally ready to leave the stage for a new generation of pop stars.

On the evidence of the Grammys, though, the truth is very different.

There was no sagging or lines on her neck

There was no hint of sag about her jowls, nor a line on her neck. Where most 45-year-olds might expect to find the unwelcome beginnings of a double chin, Madonna had the defined jawline of her friend Gwyneth Paltrow, who is a decade her junior.

Ozzy Osbourne's wife, Sharon, is firmly in the camp which believes that Madonna's seraphic new image is down to some kind of medical intervention.

Never one to call a spade a garden implement, she gasped: 'I tell you what, I went into shock at Madonna's new head.

See, she's got that Botox in that forehead. Oh, Madonna I know what you've been doing! There's not one line on that bloody head!'

I asked Mr Aposotolos Gaitanis, plastic surgeon at the Harley Cosmetic Clinic in London's Harley Street, for his opinion.

Surgeon suspects she's had cosmetic surgery

'Madonna has a very well-proportioned face, with high cheekbones, a high forehead and a well-defined jawline,' he said.

'I suspect she has been enhanced by cosmetic surgery.

'Her jawline looks less "soft" and better defined than in some earlier pictures.

This may have been done with a "mini lift", which involves the tightening of over-relaxed facial muscles and the removal of excess skin.'

And then there was the mystery of Madonna's cat-like eyes - with no hint of crinkle around them. Poor Geri Halliwell, who is 14 years younger than Madonna, looked more wrinkly than she did.

Back to Mr Gaitanis. 'A woman of 45 would usually have loose skin around this area.

'Madonna's top eyelids appear to have no excess skin whatsoever.

This could be due to an eyelift or brow lift, which involves the skin of the brow being pulled and muscles beneath tightened by keyhole surgery.

Spoofed the idea of surgery in her video

'However, I am absolutely against having to discuss it.'

'Every once in a while I see a little wrinkle on my face and I go "Bummer".

'But I'm not going to get caught up in thinking you have to stay eternally young. I am what I am.

'And I don't like the idea of someone putting you to sleep then taking knives to you.'

She even spoofed the idea of surgical intervention in her Hollywood video, in which she was shown pretending to have Botox injections.

It would surely be too brazen then - even for Madonna - to go back on her word and have her face done. Wouldn't it?

Rumours: Madonna to attend Simhastha 2004 festival?
After a ban on the sale of liquor, meat and eggs, comes a call to halt the distribution of condoms in Ujjain and remove a red-light district ahead of Simhastha 2004, to be held in April.

The once-in-12-year Kumbh congregation in the temple town attracts hundreds of thousands, and mahants and sadhus want the Pinjarwadi red-light area removed as they fear it could lead to depravity during the month-long festival.

Chief minister Uma Bharti has already declared Ujjain a “holy city”, prohibiting the sale of liquor, meat and eggs during Simhastha.

But the state-run AIDS Control Society said the presence of so many people from across the world belonging to various cultures means a safety ring is needed in Ujjain during the festival.

An office-bearer at the AIDS society who requested anonymity since his remarks could spark an adverse reaction from the “establishment” said stringent health measures were necessary.

Sadhus and mahants are up in arms for a different reason. Prakashanandji Maharaj, who represents a local akhara, said the distribution of free condoms goes against the very nature of the religious gathering.

“It will give the Hindu faith a bad name. Tell me, who will come to Kumbh with a mind to indulge in debauchery?” he asked.

The AIDS society counters by saying that the religious heads should think hard as the distribution of condoms is not intended as a reflection of one’s values. “Rather, it is in recognition of human needs that such a programme has been launched,” a campaigner said.

Swami Nirmalacharya, a sadhu from the religious town of Amarkantak, said he would not permit the sale of “evil condoms” which he said were set to “pollute” Ujjain.

Appealing to Bharti’s “good-offices”, Nirmalacharya said the West’s “crass culture” and “perverted values” should not be allowed to seep into the country. “Ujjain was a holy town, is a holy town and will continue to be a holy town,” the sadhu thundered.

AIDS campaigners said there was “nothing unusual” about the condom distribution, which was an “ongoing exercise” and should not be linked to Simhastha per se.

“It is an open secret that both pre-marital and extra-marital sex are prevalent in our society. Thus, we are just taking adequate cover,” a Bhopal-based functionary said.

“Our target is not about few thousand religious leaders but about a million participants who are going to come from all around the country and the world,” an AIDS campaigner said.

Simhastha has been dogged by several controversies.

In June 2002, a proposal by then chief minister Digvijay Singh to pick actress Aishwarya Rai as Simhastha’s brand ambassador was grounded after sadhus objected. The festival should not be reduced to a “tamasha”, they had said.

Bharti, Digvijay’s successor, was forced by the religious heads to cancel a promotional campaign handed to a multinational on the ground that it would dilute Simhastha’s “swadeshi character”.

Hollywood stars Mel Gibson, Richard Gere, Madonna and Kate Winslet are expected to attend the festival.

Yogi maintains position as rivals sue over copyright.
The normally sedate world of yoga has been rocked by a court battle between a Beverly Hills guru and rival teachers.

Bikram Choudhury, the most famous yoga master in the United States whose clients include Madonna, John McEnroe and Quincy Jones, is being sued in an intellectual property row over a 26-posture series.

Mr Choudhury said he has copyrighted the sequence of yoga postures and has sent legal letters to teachers of his technique ordering them to stop or face a lawsuit.

But other yoga teachers have hit back with their own suit claiming he has no copyright over what they claim is a 5,000-year-old tradition.

Yoga is big business in the US, drawing an estimated 18 million practitioners in the country and accounting for £15 billion in annual sales, according to Yoga Journal. The Calcutta-born yogi, who popularised the form of yoga known as Bikram, said: "Money is very important. I like money. You like money. But that’s not the end of life. Satisfaction of living, that means mental peace."

He said he has copyrighted the sequence, not the postures. He arranged the poses in a certain way, matched each pose to a precise dialogue used by the instructor and set the 90 minutes of exercises in a mirrored, carpeted room heated like a sauna. That, he says, is his intellectual property.

Now, a San Francisco nonprofit organisation of yoga enthusiasts is countering with a federal lawsuit attacking the guru’s claim that yoga is property.

They say that yoga is a tradition that cannot be owned. The suit is asking the judge to determine whether Mr Choudhury is entitled to copyright and trademark his material under federal copyright laws.

A very public falling-out.

Accident or carefully contrived publicity stunt? The brief appearance of Janet Jackson’s right nipple, complete with star-shaped piercing, may have dazzled or dismayed the more than 100 million viewers of Sunday’s Super Bowl for only 15 seconds, but the post-mortem has dragged on for days.

After the initial protests of Ms Jackson and her co-performer, Justin Timberlake, that there had been a "costume malfunction", a phrase that gave the impression that her breasts were in the possession of a dangerous android, there has been a surprising break in the ranks.

Mr Timberlake, we are now led to believe, has been cruelly tricked by an aging vamp looking for the oxygen of controversy. While fully expecting the leather corset to rip off during his duet, he had been led to belief he would be greeted by a red bra, not a bare breast. "He was duped," said a "friend" of the singer. "She’s a 37-year-old singer who’s using a hot young star to help her career." Britney Spears must be creasing herself.

Yet Justin Timberlake isn’t the only one upset by Jackson’s undressing. Tom Freston, MTV’s chief executive, whose company has been banned from organising the Super Bowl’s half-time entertainment declared: "Janet Jackson engineered it."

While her brother, Michael, has yet to stand trial, Janet has already been found guilty by the court of public opinion and may now find her career sentenced to a steady decline. One female viewer has even filed a lawsuit demanding billions of dollars in compensation for the "outrage, anger, embarrassment and serious injury" she and millions like her suffered. The vehemence of public reaction to Jackson’s "surprise" display has shocked the singer. A parallel is Roseanne Barr grabbing her crotch, in a parody of male players, before singing The Star-Spangled Banner at a televised baseball game, an action from which her career never recovered.

The reason behind Janet’s decision to flash like a Las Vegas showgirl lies deep in a childhood in which she performed rather than played. As the youngest of nine siblings - she was born on 16 May, 1966 - Janet avoided the poverty of the Jackson family’s early years but was still required to sing for her supper, joining their Las Vegas act at seven.

Yet while her older brothers and sisters filled the family coffers with their singing careers, she looked to the sound-stage and was soon a regular in sit-coms. Although her character’s name changed as she worked her way through Diff’rent Strokes, Good Time and Fame, the essentials remained the same. Janet was the shy girl next door, and when her father finally pushed her in the direction of a recording studio that was how she sounded. Her first albums, Janet Jackson (1982) and Dream Street (1984) were less than memorable, purchased by purists who already had Michael’s Thriller, then the world’s largest-selling record.

Janet was chaffing under the good-girl image and the claustrophobic influence of both her father’s management style and her mother’s faith as a Jehovah Witness. At 18, she eloped with James DeBarge, the crooner behind the hit Rhythm of the Night, but was talked back into the family fold and agreed to an annulment. The mid-western city of Minneapolis was to salvage her life and career. A music executive suggested she work with the producers Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis in the city that produced Prince, and she agreed over her father’s objections.

Joseph Jackson’s concern was astute. Janet made an album called Control that detailed her past life, then fired him as manager. A 15-year feud followed, but her career flourished. Control went platinum, her videos, choreographed by Paula Abdul, were acclaimed, and suddenly Michael faced a challenge.

If in the 1980s she reinvented herself as a powerful singer, the 1990s witnessed an even greater surprise, when she challenged Madonna as the queen of erotica. Her 1993 album Janet featured her topless on the cover with her breasts cradled by a man’s hands. (The unseen figure was songwriter Rene Elizondo. Their marriage was secret and would only become public knowledge when they divorced seven years later.) At the time she explained to Rolling Stone: "For the first time I’m feeling free. I love feeling deeply sexual - and don’t mind letting the world know." Over the next decade, as Madonna became a mother and adopted a more matronly pose - apart from the occasional lesbian clinch for MTV - Janet Jackson borrowed her discarded stockings and suspender belt.

The sexuality of Jackson’s lyrics was cranked up a notch with the release of The Velvet Rope in 1997 (which also charted her emotional breakdown and battle with depression), but the culmination came in 2000 with the release of All For You, on the cover of which she posed naked but for a white fur rug. On the album she took a few swipes at Elizondo, who had sued her for millions, but on tour the emphasis remained on the sex. While Madonna had danced raunchily with her - mainly gay - male dancers, Janet took to providing a male audience member with a public lap-dance.

Sex coupled with carefully crafted pop tunes will certainly sell, and Janet Jackson has been the principal beneficiary. In 1996 Virgin Records paid her $80 million, which pushed her past her elder brother to take the title of the music industry’s highest-paid performer. Her most recent deal with the label was for $90 million. Like Madonna, Janet Jackson has been unable to resist the occasional return to films, taking roles in John Singleton’s Poetic Justice and, with Eddie Murphy, in The Nutty Professor II: The Klumps. Her Oscar nomination, however, was for Best Song, After in Poetic Justice. She didn’t win.

The curiosity of Jackson’s career is that despite her provocative poses and explicit lyrics she has, perhaps until now, retained the image of the family’s most wholesome member. Stacked against accusations of paedophilia made against her brother, and the decision of her sister, LaToya, to strip for Playboy, there can be little wonder. Yet the reaction of the American public against her Super Bowl strip is such that this can no longer be guaranteed and her record label is genuinely concerned.

"Do you want this? Can you handle this?" sang Janet in You Want This, an old hit. This time the answer from the American public would appear to be a resounding: "No."

Women over 35 'Worry More About Looks Than Security'
Women over 35 worry more about losing their looks as they grow older than having future financial security, a study showed today.

Half the women surveyed said they felt “dissatisfied” with how they look for their age while one in seven worried more about their beauty as they aged, rather than their bank balance.

While society regards older men as “distinguished”, according to the survey by anti-ageing expert RoC, women felt the pressure to look and feel young.

Despite the findings, British women still value their years of wisdom and experience over their looks.

Less than one-third of women, or 31%, aged over 35 would want to go back 10 years and “do it all again” to retain their youthful appearance, although nearly 60% would be happy to stay the age they are now without wrinkles.

Resorting to elective cosmetic surgery was not the answer for 88%, who said they would not chose to go under the knife to look less worn.

Only 7% looked at cosmetic surgery to keep them looking young and 36% preferred to use skincare products.

Madonna’s surgery-free approach to beauty was preferable for 31% of women and while more than 21% were “in awe” of Cher’s cosmetic surgery, they would not take such action themselves.

Only 5% of those surveyed approved of Kim Cattrall’s Sex and the City character Samantha’s face peel and botox approach to looking good.

While women worry about their looks, only 12% would give up a current pleasure such as smoking, drinking or sunbathing in order to look younger.

One in 10 said the love of a younger man might do the trick and would take up a toy boy to feel younger.

Christine Webber, psychotherapist and life-coach, said: “Although most females hope to live to a ripe old age, they want to look great while they’re doing so or they won’t feel confident mentally.

“There are huge demands on women socially and in the work place to stay youthful and it can be an enormous pressure to live up to society’s expectations.”

She added: “The solution can be drastic measures such as cosmetic surgery – but many realise that an operation may leave you looking like someone else, rather than simply ten years younger.”

Regionally, Welsh women were the most fearful about looking haggard with nearly one in five, or 17%, citing this as their main concern in old age.

In East Anglia and Wales, 16% of women would rather seek out a toy boy than visit the surgeon’s table.

Northerners and Londoners were the most confident about their looks with 58% believing they looked younger than their age.

Only 7% of Scottish women would consider giving up smoking, drinking or sunbathing compared to 20% in London.

One in 10 women admitted they looked older than their age in the east Midlands. But women in the same region were the only ones more worried about their careers than their looks with 21% citing career and 14% looks as their main concern.

Some 29% of women in the south west preferred Cher’s surgical approach to anti ageing compared with 15% preferring Madonna’s more natural ageing process.

Half of women surveyed in London were “happy” with how they looked for their age, and 12%, or one in eight, would give up a habit like smoking, drinking or sunbathing to improve their looks.

The research was carried out by ACCESS, the Omnibus Division at BMRB International, in January 2004 with a sample of 759 UK women aged 35 and over.

Signed Madonna cowboy hat auctioned.

A motley crew of South Florida characters and hoi polloi turned up at criminal defense lawyer Roy Black’s house for his and his wife Lea’s annual black-tie bash in support of Bay Point School, which serves at-risk youths.

A new translation of the Zohar, the primary Kabbalah text.
Here's how the Zohar, the bible of Jewish mysticism, opens its discussion of Genesis 1:1, the story of creation: "A spark of impenetrable darkness flashed within the concealed of the concealed, from the head of Infinity–-a cluster of vapor forming in formlessness, thrust in a ring, not white, not black, not red, not green, no color at all. As a cord surveyed, it yielded radiant colors. Deep within the spark gushed a flow, splaying colors below, concealed within the concealed of the mystery of Ein Sof."

If you think you understand, you definitely don't. It's simply impossible for the non-expert to make much sense of kabbalistic symbolism, which is recondite beyond measure. Yet in his new, three-volume translation of the Zohar, the primary kabbalistic text, scholar Daniel Matt has done an amazing job of making Kabbalah as accessible as it can be.

Matt, who taught theology in Berkeley, Calif. until taking on the Zohar project for Stanford University Press, retains a claim to authenticity that is not common in the pop version of kabbalah. For many who claim to understand kabbalah, the "cluster of vapor forming in formlessness" has yielded not a concealed mystery but a product line: "Kabbalah Cures: Headache Relief Ointment," made with "pure Kabbalah Water & Essential Oils"; red Kabbalah strings for $26 each, as worn on the left wrist by Madonna and other stars; and all manner of other Kabbalah "gear" on sale from the website of the Kabbalah Centre based in Los Angeles.

But turning from Britney Spears on the cover of Entertainment Weekly wearing a bustier and red string to Matt's monumentally serious and austere Zohar translation, you breathe an entirely different kind of air.

What is the Zohar, exactly? In three volumes that stretch altogether to encyclopedic length, it is, in theory, a commentary on the first five books of the Bible. Some of it reads not entirely unlike any of the ancient Jewish books of midrash. In these works, the ancient rabbis comment and expand upon the terse Biblical text, which is understood to conceal layer upon layer of secret, hidden meanings.

Other parts of the Zohar read like an odd peripatetic novel, as a group of 2nd-century Jewish sages, led by Rabbi Shimon ben Yohai, travel about the Galilee in northern Israel, discussing the Torah. Still other parts have no narrative framework and expound, without pretending to be a commentary, on the interrelationship among Ein Sof and the ten sefirot.

The expression Ein Sof, meaning "without end" in Hebrew, is the mystic designation of the unnamable source of all being. Ein Sof is not God, exactly–-rather, "it" is "God" as he relates to us humans, somehow emerging into our universe in stages called sefirot, derived from Ein Sof.

When the Zohar is in its more conventionally midrashic mode, it's possible, thanks to Matt's substantial footnotes, to have some vague notion of what the book is getting at. There are some very interesting and haunting images. When I was working on a biography of the patriarch Abraham, for example, I came across the ancient tradition, originating in the Zohar, that the Cave of Machpelah in Hebron is somehow a portal to paradise--the Garden of Eden.

Brit itch.

Madonna, Gwyneth and Jennifer Connelly each has one. It’s official – the British man is the hottest accessory du jour, drools LUISA LIM. 

FOR aeons the British man has suffered the stereotype of being pasty-faced, orthodontically challenged asexuals, but now he has emerged as the hottest must-have.  

A few years ago, it was the Aussie male who was setting hearts aflutter worldwide due to hot Aussie exports like Russell Crowe, Heath Ledger and Hugh Jackman. American actresses (Meg Ryan, Heather Graham) all had, or at least really wanted a bit of a go at some antipodean love, but that love has seriously waned in favour of pasty-faced colonialists. My theory is that while Aussie stars may be hot, they tend not to portray Australians, therefore not doing much to advertise the undoubtedly many charms of the Aussie male. The British male, however, is well represented in acting and fiction – both cinematic and literary.  

The British man is to the celebrity dating scene what the Atkins diet was to eating habits. Madonna, mother of all trendsetters, started the fad by nabbing her British hubby, Guy Ritchie, and proceeded to embrace all things English. Jamie Lee Curtis was a lot smarter and scored hers ages ago – a lord no less – Lord Christopher Haden-Guest, and Jennifer Connelly tied the knot with A Beautiful Mind co-star Paul Bettany.

And, of course, the most recent Britboy devotee, Gwyneth Paltrow, finally got over her bad luck with British men (in 2002, she moaned that Brit guys never asked her out) and has snagged Coldplay’s Chris Martin and they are having a baby too. Yes, Gwyneth, who has dated Brad and Ben and is so fussy that she has a macrobiotic diet (no wheat, no dairy, no taste), has decided wedded bliss lies with an Englishman. And whatever the celebs do, you can bet “civilians” will soon follow – note the celeb factor regarding the Atkins diet and Pilates exercises.  

So what makes them so hot? There has been the “hotness” of Brit lit (Tolkien, Austen), huge surprise successes of British movies showing British actors in swoon-worthy roles (Bridget Jones’s Diary, Notting Hill, Love Actually) or big movies with dishy British actors in them – Orlando Bloom in The Lord of the Rings, Jude Law in anything (or nothing). And Brits are so sexy as villains. But perhaps it is also the repackaging of “Britishness” as being to-die-for.  

What’s hot 

  • The accent – I know, I know, there are lots of different accents in the British Isles but for the sake of argument, the British male that is being sold worldwide is Richard Curtis’s (writer/producer of Notting Hill, Love Actually) idea of the British man. And who is complaining when it looks and sounds like Colin Firth? That public-schooled, Oxbridge-educated accent just screams intelligence.  

  • They are not mushy. Unlike the “let’s share innards” openness that Americans have, British men have that stoical stiff-upper-lipped demeanour that is so totally hot. When Mark Darcy tells Bridget that he likes her just as she is, it is just plain sexy. Plus, when they tell you little things like that, you know it means so much more than someone else showering you with verbal love diarrhoea.  

  • They just seem so much more cultured and have been brought up in a world where Shakespeare, Dickens, Tolkien, Bronte et al are in the ether.  

  • The sense of humour – witty, self-deprecating and drier than the Sahara – what’s not to love?

What’s not 

  • Pasty face. What can you expect if you live in a practically sunless country?  

  • A superiority complex. Well, they did have an empire. C’mon, if you were British, you’d probably be a little proud too.

So now that Madge and Gwynnie have started the ball rolling, will the flock follow as they did with the Atkins diet and butt-crunching Pilates? Can we expect planeloads of desperate foreign singletons disembarking in a haze of duty-free perfume at Heathrow intent on getting a souvenir that will last till death do they part?  

All I can say is that I’m glad I did the marital equivalent of the stock market adage and “bought low”, but fortunately I have absolutely no intention of selling high. Happy hunting, ladies!

More on Madonna Madonna fighting to protect her Wiltshire property.

We all dream of hiding ourselves away in a home set in acres of countryside, but if you happen to be one of the most famous faces in the world, the need for privacy must be a priority. When Madonna and her English husband Guy Ritchie bought the Ashcombe House estate in Wiltshire, the one thing she might have hoped to keep at bay were members of the public. Instead, the American star has found herself in a dispute with ramblers who, under new legislation, are demanding the right to roam across parts of her 1,200-acre estate. Although the Countryside Agency has agreed that walkers will be denied access to some 15 acres, Madonna wants to place other parts of the estate off-limits to the public. The issue will now be resolved through a public inquiry.

For most buyers it is not so much the controversy over the right to roam on open land that will be engage them, but the age-old issue of public rights of way. Footpaths have a habit of popping up in all sorts of spots, and they are not confined to large estates. Owners of a cottage that may once have been part of a shortcut to nip between farm and pub could find weekend walkers passing right under their windows.

William Kirkland of Cluttons estate agents explains: "There is no doubt that where people are spending a considerable amount of money, they would rather not have the complications of footpaths. Of course, it depends on where they are situated, but even those that are apparently not well-used can change just like that."

That could well be the case for the footpath that runs across part of Madonna's property, he says. "The house sits in a bowl and when you walk up the valley the path comes to within a few hundred yards of the front before veering off."

Rupert Bradstock of buying agency Property Vision is familiar with the sensitivities of the famous, who often rule out anywhere with public rights of way within distance of a powerful camera lens. "Other purchasers are rather more specific. They might reject anything gardenside, but be perfectly happy if a path close to a house is screened by woodland."

James Laing, head of the rural and residential division of Strutt & Parker, is himself used to strange faces. His Berkshire farmhouse in 80 acres is wrapped around on three sides by either a footpath or bridle path. Walkers and riders are as much a part of his landscape as the animals, and he has few complaints. "They almost always stop and say good morning and ask what we are doing. They have a genuine interest in the countryside. In every respect it is an enjoyable experience. Some of the footpaths do run close to the main building, one within 40 yards of the house. You can effectively walk right through the farm buildings and see the house over the garden hedge. The land is open there with no gates or stiles. Our main worry is security."

All the same, Laing believes landowners should be more welcoming. "If we create proper parking areas at the start of footpaths, there will no reason for people to stray on to grazing land. How often do you see a path start on a narrow verge in the middle of bramble hedge and with nowhere to park for half a mile? Then walkers roam illegally across field until they pick up the route. Worse still, they are likely to block up gateways with their cars. My problem is not with ramblers but with motorcyclists. They can be physically and verbally abusive if you ask them to leave."

Off-road motorcyclists and four-wheel-drive owners are often accused of damaging large tracts of open land in many parts of the country. But Henry Holland-Hibbert, who heads the country house department at Lane Fox, believes fears about the extent of bad behaviour are exaggerated.

"Only about two per cent of people drop litter or bring motorbikes on to bridleways. The remaining 98 per cent are lovely, often telling owners if they have spotted something wrong or seen an animal in distress. But privacy is a big issue and it was a great shame that when changes were made to the right to roam, the national network of footpaths was not modernised at the same time. A huge opportunity was missed."

Whether rights of way affect the value of a property is harder to say, according to estate agents. When a cottage on the Devon coast with a footpath next to it sold for 15 per cent less than its path-free neighbour, that had to be a factor. But it is not unusual for public rights of way to be an advantage, particularly if farms have been turned to other uses. In Goring Heath, Oxfordshire, FPDSavills is selling Bottom House Farm, a Grade II-listed farmhouse with a bridlepath running down the driveway.

In the opinion of Vanessa Townsend, from the selling agents, it could be better screened from the house, but its great plus is that the property comes with a paddock adjoining the bridlepath so you can avoid any roads when you ride out. The brick-and-flint house comes with more than three acres - and a rare colony of glow-worms. It has a guide price of £900,000.

Savills is also selling Home Farm, Ulverscroft in Leicestershire, which has three cottages currently let. The public has footpath access but also areas of woodland set aside for their use under the national forestry planting scheme. The asking price is £1.5 million.

In Kent, properties close to "toll rides" - a network of off-road tracks for horses that primarily provide links between public rights of way - are of increasing interest to riders. Subscribers to the scheme have miles of countryside at their disposal.

After fire destroyed the original house at Summerhill near Tenterden in Kent six years ago, the owners rebuilt it using local craftsmen and materials. Now a grand country house with all modern conveniences is sitting among established gardens with views across parkland and countryside. These are shared by the public using a footpath that crosses a small section of the land, a fair distance from the house. Alexander Hunt from Cluttons, who is selling the property at a guide price of £3.4 million, says that buyers regard the well-marked path with oak stiles as something of a feature.

It is just as well they have been so sanguine, because moving a path can be complicated and is often unsuccessful. Former Rolling Stone Bill Wyman did succeed at his property in Suffolk; how Madonna fares in her appeal to preserve her open land will become clear in a matter of weeks.
