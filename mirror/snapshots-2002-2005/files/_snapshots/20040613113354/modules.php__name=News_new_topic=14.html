Madonna gets her man

dailynewstranscript
By Bob Tremblay / News Staff Writer
Thursday, May 13, 2004

During more than three decades as a producer, Michael Seligman has amassed enough anecdotes to fill a book or two. Or three.

    While he can't reveal most of them, Seligman does recall one incident involving Madonna he can relate.

    In 1990, the year of "Dick Tracy," Madonna is scheduled to sing the film's Oscar-nominated song "Sooner or Later (I Always Get My Man)" during the "Academy Awards" broadcast.

    "And she worked her tail off for that song," recalled Seligman, who served as the show's supervising producer. "She worked with a choreographer. She worked for three weeks straight every day, day and night, refining this number. She's a perfectionist."

    The problem is not everything works perfectly during a live broadcast.

    "We rehearsed everything and it went fine," said Seligman.

    The production calls for Madonna to rise from below the stage on a lift through a hole in the stage floor. Once Madonna is on stage, a stage engineer in the rafters will be instructed to switch on her microphone and she'll sing.

    "So it's time for Madonna to sing and the stage manager with his headset on tells the stage engineer to switch her mike on. But nothing happens. He repeats himself. Nothing happens. The mike doesn't go on," said Seligman.

    "So the stage manager tells Madonna in this nervous kind of voice that the stage engineer is not answering his call and her mike isn't being put on. 'But don't worry,' he said. 'You'll be handed a mike when you get up on stage. It'll be fine.'"

    Madonna apparently disagreed.

    "As the lift is about to go up, she grabs the stage manager by the neck," said Seligman. "Now the lift is going up and the stage manager is going up by his throat. She finally drops him, grabs the mike and does the number perfectly."

    Seligman later learns that the heat from the lights in the rafters affected the engineer and he fell asleep. "He looks for me in the truck after the number and I told him that stuff happens," said the producer. "We became friends after that."

    And the song that Madonna sang? It won the Oscar.

Starter Kit: Madonna

Interesting article from BELFASTELEGRAPH.co.uk:

Madonna doesn't just ride the zeitgeist, she grabs it, reins it in and tames it, leaving her many critics dumbfounded by her ability to stretch her success into yet another area.
By Mike Ivers
belfasttelegraph.co.uk

30 April 2004
LIKE A VIRGIN (1984)

It was on her second album that Madonna took hold of the world, with a finely judged play for the iconic sexuality and catchy pop that has continued to define her appeal to males and females. If this was all that Madonna had ever had to give, we may not still celebrate her. The light and fluffy pop of Like a Virgin, Material Girl, Over and Over, and Dress You Up made it clear that the music was more about dancing than singing. However, pop has never needed to define itself too carefully, and these songs were solid examples of what was happening in New York dance clubs at that time.


TRUE BLUE (1986)

Madonna established herself as the biggest female artist in the world with the release of her third album, True Blue. It was the biggest selling record of the year, topping the UK album chart for six consecutive weeks and going straight to No 1 in every country it was released in. True Blue is Madonna's first 'grown-up' album and the first to truly stand the test of time. She attempts something different with every song and carries it off with some aplomb. The singles are the standouts, particularly the timeless Papa Don't Preach, and La Isla Bonita, but there are no obvious fillers like there were on previous albums.


LIKE A PRAYER (1989)

More than any other artist, Madonna has pushed the envelope of controversy to boost her celebrity - she knew exactly what she was doing when she combined Catholic guilt and soft pornography in her Like A Prayer videos. However, the celebrity alone would have gained her no ground at all without an astonishing musical judgment. Like a Prayer is easily Madonna's most mature effort of the 1980s. It dispensed with the dance tracks and concentrated instead on melody and structure. It's the album that proved Madonna could make believable, heartfelt, adult pop music.


RAY OF LIGHT (1998)

This lush, sad and spiritual album kept Madonna in front of the popular acceptance of electronic dance music in a way no-one thought she would manage. Ray of Light was Madonna's first album of new material since 1994's Bedtime Stories, and her first since motherhood. It is also her most accomplished record yet. William Orbit's tasteful sonic constructions provided a hip musical backdrop, and although the lyrics do overreach themselves, few people listen to Madonna for the words. This pinnacle production gained the singer her first ever Grammy, and set a new benchmark for danceable pop

Italians do it better

LAweekly published an intersting article on Dj duo Camaione and Cilione

Claudio Camaione, in a black suit, Converse sneakers and a cappello alla siciliana hat, mixes raspberry Cosmopolitans and smokes cigarettes, while Paolo Cilione, dressed in similar attire except for a Borsalino hat, spins their latest dance remix. Here in their Silver Lake basement music studio, a witty, candlelit space decorated with disco balls, mannequins, guitars and a red-leather psychiatrist’s couch, the two spend days and nights producing, directing, editing and developing electronic dance music, short films and music videos

So far, they’ve completed two albums under the name Blow-Up , including Exploding Plastic Pleasure, which features vocals by Dee Dee Ramone, Blondie’s Deborah Harry and spoken-word performer/chanteuse Lydia Lunch, as well as a number of video shorts such as “Fly With Me,” starring Debbie Mazar, a sci-fi account of a white mechanical dove that escapes from a boy’s hands and takes a Rear Window–esque journey through L.A.’s club scene. Blow-Up started as an underground word-of-mouth venture but quickly caught the attention of Madonna, who called on Cilione and Camaione to remix a couple of singles for her, including the recently released “Love Profusion.”

The pair met nine years ago in Milan at a fashion show. At the time, Camaione was deejaying at clubs and private parties, after spending his younger days modeling for Moschino and dancing in music videos. Cilione, who grew up in Brooklyn, was an ’80s club kid, then a photographer, painter and actor. In the ’90s he landed in London and worked as a publishing assistant, club promoter and deejay.

After a short spell in New York, the duo moved to L.A. and started to slowly build their studio one piece of equipment at a time. “We would save up and go mix a song,” says Cilione. Today, thanks to Madonna and others, the artists’ studio is fully loaded. “L.A. is easy,” notes Cilione. “You can always find ways to make extra cash.”

'AMERICAN' SALES

Dear Keith,

I'm hoping that you can help me with the recent sales figures for Madonna's "American Life" album and "Remixed & Revisited" EP. Madonna is one of the few elite who has earned two Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) diamond awards, for "Like a Virgin" and "The Immaculate Collection." Thank you for your time and expertise.

Loc D. Tra

Hi Loc,

So far, "American Life" and "Remixed & Revisited" have sold 630,000 and 85,000 units, respectively, in the United States, according to Nielsen SoundScan.

Madonna has two diamond certified albums, for "Like a Virgin" and "The Immaculate Collection." A Diamond award indicates shipments of 10 million units to U.S. retailers. The certification and awards process is administered by the RIAA. More information on the process can be found at the organization's Web site,riaa.com.)

Of the remaining albums in Madonna's catalog, the most likely candidate for diamond status is "True Blue." Currently, the album is certified as having shipped seven million units to U.S. retailers.

Read It First Here: Story Of The Year.

Believe it or not, there’s more to St. Louis than Nelly. The city is also home to Story Of The Year, who were scouted by Goldfinger frontman John Feldmann for Maverick Records. Feldmann also worked closely with the band in producing their major label debut, Page Avenue. ChartAttack sat down with Dan Marsala and Josh Wills to talk smack.

How do you feel about being affiliated with Madonna?
Dan Marsala: Madonna’s a cool lady. We haven’t met her actually. She doesn’t really do much with the label, she just owns it. Her partner does all the other stuff. Guy Oseary, he’s the actual guy at Maverick. We haven’t met her, so it’s not like she really is our boss, but she really is.


Story Of The Year

What band would you like to beat up?
Josh Wills: The Baha Men. DM: I would have to say Disturbed, for doing that "Oo-wah-ah-ah-ah!" voice.
JW: I’d like to change my answer to Staind. Mostly the singer because he’s so sad. He just needs to get his ass kicked. He stands up on stage and the whole band’s rockin’ and he’s just… I don’t get it.

What was it like working with John Feldmann (of Goldfinger)?
DM: It was good. He was cool. I’ve liked Goldfinger since I was 15, so I thought it was really cool. He’s a cool dude. It was weird, it was just in a little bedroom studio in his house. Not much to it, but he gets good sounds and we had a good time. It was fun.

Have you experienced any backlash from fans after signing to Maverick?
JW: That happens here and there. I’ve read things where people say we’ve sold out, but you can’t sell out if you haven’t done anything in the first place, you know what I mean? They’re just people who don’t really know what they’re talking about. They’re selfish with their own music, they don’t want bands to get big just because they don’t want them to, and then they get pissed off when one of their favourite bands has more fans than they think they should. It’s just dumb stuff like that. I don’t really care. I just read it and laugh.

How do you feel about people downloading your music?
JW: It’s more about people liking our music and knowing who we are than money and record sales.
DM: The shows are where it’s at, so as long as the kids are there it’s all good.

MANDONNA.

What's the big idea? It used to be simple: Form a band, write some original material, build up a grassroots following and then hope to maybe someday open for Third Eye Blind at the Last Day Saloon. But the shrewd dudes behind Mandonna realized you can just skip right past all that nonsense by mining one of the most lucrative hit-making reserves of the past three decades: Madonna's back catalog. Add a couple of glow sticks and some svelte dancers in wedding dresses, and the all-male, all-live tribute to the Material Girl that started out last year is now one of the most lively, funny and utterly irresistible gigs in town.

The players: Vocalists Michael Miller and Mark Edwards, drummer Victor Andrade, guitarist Jeff Suffin, bassist Charles Moto, keyboardist Jason Goodman and backup vocalists and dancers Tony Jenkins and Hugh Groman.

Why they think the world needs an all-male Madonna tribute band: "There couldn't be a better person to pay tribute to -- someone who is more popular, who has more breadth of music or is more fun," Groman says.

Is Mandonna a man or a band? Excellent question. It turns out it's a band, made up mostly of veteran musicians who have been knocking around the Bay Area club scene for years. The backup singers and dancers were brought in to provide the requisite exhibitionist factor involved in all things surrounding Madonna. "They're the eye candy," Andrade says.

Why it's easier getting a show playing Madonna's music than their own: "I've been in the club scene for 10 years playing originals with little glimmer of hope," Andrade says. "When Mandonna played its first show, the room was totally packed and everyone was screaming, going nuts. Bam, it worked. We haven't had a bad show yet." The credit goes not only to the songs that have ingrained themselves into peoples' heads like inoperable brain tumors but also to the inherent ridiculousness of the whole concept. "People hear about Mandonna and instantly think, 'What a great idea,' " Groman says.

Why they don't think it's that hard to channel a yoga-practicing, Kaballah-endorsing, bad-movie-making multimillionaire pop star and mother of two: "Because we're not channeling Madonna," Groman says.

Why not? "Having a male sound like Madonna is going to be tough," Andrade reasons. "It's Mandonna. It's a male vocalist singing a male part of a Madonna song. That's the ticket. It seems comical, but we're all serious musicians, and it's a serious band." Which may explain why sometimes it sounds more as if they're channeling Linkin Park -- singing "Like a Virgin."

What does Madonna think of Mandonna? Not much. She's never heard of it.

The Madonna song that speaks to the members of Mandonna most: "It's hard to pick a favorite," Groman says. "They're all fun and have such a great energy." The rest of the band has no problem going for "Like a Prayer," which it usually saves for the end, using a piped-in chorus and rocking guitar solo. "That's always our encore," Andrade says.

What they consider the best Madonna phase: Definitely not the whole rap- and-latte "American Life" period, and not so much the overwrought "Erotica" era. "I remember when 'Like a Virgin' came out and she performed it on the Video Music Awards in that wedding dress," Groman says. "It was such a big deal. It was huge." The early years it is, then.

How often the backup dancers get to wear wedding dresses onstage: Just once so far. "We did it for Halloween in the Castro last year," Groman says.

How long it took to get the choreography down: With the wealth of Madonna viewing material to draw from, it's still a work in progress. But the band did bring in a choreographer to help the dancers re-create some key moves from Madonna's most famous videos. "There's a lot of inspiration there," Groman says.

What it feels like for a girl: Mandonna has no idea. That's one of the Madonna songs they haven't learned yet -- primarily because when she plays it live she does it in Spanish.

What they think Madonna would be like if she were a man: "Madonna has very androgynous energy," Groman says. "She's somebody who has qualities of both male and female. She's so assertive and one of the most famous performers in the world. She seems to transcend a lot of things." Gender is so passe.

Vancouver high-school gigs led to deal with Madonna.

Dakona doesn't mind being the underdog. In fact, the Langley-based band is used to it.

"When we got signed to Maverick, we got a decent deal," says vocalist Ryan McAllister, interviewed at a downtown coffee bar. "And then Lillix signed for a far less substantial amount and they were front-page news on all the papers."

"And it was nearly a year after our signing," says guitarist Brook Winstanley. "The headlines said 'First-ever Canadian band signed to Madonna's Maverick Records, Lillix,' and we're like 'What about us? We've been signed for eight months! We heard about them thinking about Lillix when we were sitting in the offices signing the papers!' "

That a big deal wasn't made out of Dakona's signing might prove beneficial, since it means the story is still fresh enough to accompany the group's years-in-the-making Maverick debut, Perfect Change. Forged from sing-along choruses and frat-friendly soft/loud dynamics, the songs are low on angst for all of their modern-rock sheen.

Recording the album turned out to be a bit of a nightmare, however.

"It involved a lot of ups and downs," McAllister says. "A lot of these songs we knew inside out. And when we went to Toronto we started overthinking stuff." After five months in Hogtown with Our Lady Peace overseer Arnold Lanni, the singer and his fellow Dakonians realized they "were so inside of it we didn't really know what was good or bad". After a two-week break they returned to the studio to find the tracks didn't have the magic they wanted.

"There were a few songs that hit it," Winstanley says. "But on other songs we realized we just needed to get in a room and play them. A song that takes three minutes to run through live shouldn't take three months to record."

An example of a recording that went right is "Soul for Sale", the album's most straight-ahead rock track, which was laid down as an afterthought in Seattle. "Certain producers vibe off certain songs and not on others, and Arnold wasn't vibing on that one. He thought it sounded too much like Rick Springfield." Wisely, the band decided to do it themselves and recorded the song in a mere 12 hours.

With the record in the bag and a hometown show at Richard's on Richards on Wednesday (February 18), Dakona is ready to reclaim the title of Madonna's first Canadian discovery. However, the question isn't so much why the signing was ignored as how the quartet got there in the first place, without any local press or radio attention. And the answer is solid, old-fashioned dues-paying that saw the band build a buzz by, as McAllister puts it, "playing everywhere between here and Salmon Arm".

"We gigged a lot of high schools in the [Fraser] valley," says Winstanley, recalling the first half of the five-year-old group's career.

McAllister adds: "It's the biggest untapped resource for independent bands. Don't worry about getting your 10 percent of your door cut at the Pic when it turns out to be about 20 bucks. Go and play for free, for 500 captive people who never get entertainment for lunch hour. These kids just sit in the cafeteria and talk about Survivor. Give 'em something to see."

Glimmer of hope for music industry?

The annual Midem music industry gathering in Cannes, France, has over the last few years been lamenting the possible demise of the industry, thanks to file-sharing networks and piracy. In.Tech reporter STEVEN PATRICK, on his annual hunt for free wine, women and song, smelled a sea change at this year's meet. 

Just as the free market was preparing to lower the casket on the music industry, a combination of lawsuits against illegal downloaders, a growing legal online market and rising ringtone sales is giving the industry fresh hope. 

The industry had been declining steadily for the past few years – CD sales fell 2% last year – which it has blamed on increased illegal downloading. 

However, 2004 began with some sense of optimism as album sales rose 18.2% in the first week of the new year, compared to the corresponding period the previous year. 

This optimism was evident at the 38th edition of the world’s largest music festival Midem, which took place on January 25-29 in Cannes. 

“A lot of people were nervous and scared the last couple of years, but now they are beginning to (accept the fact that) the old model is dying,” said prog rock veteran and world music icon Peter Gabriel, who made a brief stop at Midem to announce a new artiste initiative.

“We’ve got a lot of new possibilities and we’re excited about what we can do in this more digital world, and we really want a lot of artistes to explore it,” he said. 

EMI (US) senior vice-president of digital development and distribution Ted Cohen, who chaired MidemNet, began by saying that online downloading was still a problem but had become more manageable. 

“We’re turning the corner. There’s a real business in front of this saga. We’re here to franchise the disenfranchised. We want to increase the amount of music available online,” he said. 

Yet one still cannot purchase songs by The Beatles online. Artistes like Madonna and Radiohead won’t allow single song downloads, the latter arguing that its songs are an integral part of a conceptual whole – the album.

“We have to free up more catalogue, making more songs available (online),” said Cohen. “We want to learn how to embrace P2P (peer-to-peer) technology, which is not a bad thing if it is 'monetised' and the artistes get paid. We’re here to talk about the issues, the roadblocks and the opportunities.” 

The music industry is still trying to create an online legal market as illegal downloading subsides in the United States. The reason why it’s taking so long is still the same – a labyrinth of copyright issues. 

EMI Music executive vice-president John Rose said, “The challenge we face in moving forward is getting all the rights holders in the chain, not just the record labels.”

Mobiles telephones could be lifeline the music business needs.

Mobile telephones could be the unlikely lifeline that music superstars such as Britney Spears, Madonna and Justin Timberlake need to claw back some of the vast amounts of cash lost to illegal Internet downloads, industry insiders say.

"Mobiles + music = money" was one of the messages being aired at the global music industry's MIDEM trade fair taking place here, echoing hopes that the burgeoning mobile music sector could come to the rescue of artists and the worldwide record industry.

"There is no doubt that music is a fundamental element of the mobile business," said Ralph Simon, chairman of the first ever MIDEM mobile music forum. With 1.5 billion mobile phone handsets currently in use globally, "This is a huge, huge opportunity for the music industry -- for artists, managers, labels, publishers and even retailers," he said.

A staggering 113 billion SMS (text) messages were sent via portable handsets around the world in 2003, Simon noted, saying this was evidence of how big the mobile business could be for music.

The telecoms industry is already well geared up to tap into the huge revenue potential offered by the mass of people addicted to mobiles.

Ringtones are already big business, particularly with young mobile phone users, who are ready to pay to change their tone regularly, with the latest hit track from Beyonce or Jennifer Lopez on their mobiles. Ironically, members of the same age group are among the biggest offenders in illegally downloading music.

Today ringtones sales worldwide are on the up and up, even threatening to outstrip sales of CD singles, currently the music world's biggest sellers. Ringtones are so popular that hit charts are now emerging -- Japan has a top-40 mobile ringtones chart and Britain is due to role out a version this year. In the United States, rapper 50 Cent scooped a platinum award for the millionth download of his smash hit "In The Club".

Ringtones however are not the only bright prospect for the music industry, said Simon, citing events in Japan.

Giant Japanese telecoms operator NTT DoCoMo's has signed up just over 40 million paying subscribers in the country and 1.5 million outside in just five years by offering a simple entertainment-based service, senior DoCoMo's executive Takeshi Natsuno said at MIDEM.

"Japan has proved what is possible," said Natsuno, the founder and brains behind DoCoMo's I-mode service, insisting that it was not true to say that Japanese people were different or that Japan was a special market. "People in Europe like downloading ringtones and playing games just as much as people in Japan."

"Music and games were the reasons for I-mode's success", Natsuno stressed, as long as prices remained reasonable and the technology easy to use.

Boosted sales from the world of telecoms would be welcome to world of music, which is in dire need of a shot in the arm, with CD sales down 10 percent 2003.

Although the next generation of mobile phones should enable users to download whole tracks with vastly superior sound quality, the music industry is not quite ready to take that turning. Major copyright issues still remain and artists as well as record labels want to be sure the technology is well protected from pirates.

Some executives are also cautioning that ringtones are not the only answer to solving the music industry's doldrums.

"Ringtones are important, but they aren't the big saviour some people think," EMI Music Publishing UK chairman and CEO Peter Reichardt was recently quoted as saying.
