Mobiles telephones could be lifeline the music business needs.

Mobile telephones could be the unlikely lifeline that music superstars such as Britney Spears, Madonna and Justin Timberlake need to claw back some of the vast amounts of cash lost to illegal Internet downloads, industry insiders say.

"Mobiles + music = money" was one of the messages being aired at the global music industry's MIDEM trade fair taking place here, echoing hopes that the burgeoning mobile music sector could come to the rescue of artists and the worldwide record industry.

"There is no doubt that music is a fundamental element of the mobile business," said Ralph Simon, chairman of the first ever MIDEM mobile music forum. With 1.5 billion mobile phone handsets currently in use globally, "This is a huge, huge opportunity for the music industry -- for artists, managers, labels, publishers and even retailers," he said.

A staggering 113 billion SMS (text) messages were sent via portable handsets around the world in 2003, Simon noted, saying this was evidence of how big the mobile business could be for music.

The telecoms industry is already well geared up to tap into the huge revenue potential offered by the mass of people addicted to mobiles.

Ringtones are already big business, particularly with young mobile phone users, who are ready to pay to change their tone regularly, with the latest hit track from Beyonce or Jennifer Lopez on their mobiles. Ironically, members of the same age group are among the biggest offenders in illegally downloading music.

Today ringtones sales worldwide are on the up and up, even threatening to outstrip sales of CD singles, currently the music world's biggest sellers. Ringtones are so popular that hit charts are now emerging -- Japan has a top-40 mobile ringtones chart and Britain is due to role out a version this year. In the United States, rapper 50 Cent scooped a platinum award for the millionth download of his smash hit "In The Club".

Ringtones however are not the only bright prospect for the music industry, said Simon, citing events in Japan.

Giant Japanese telecoms operator NTT DoCoMo's has signed up just over 40 million paying subscribers in the country and 1.5 million outside in just five years by offering a simple entertainment-based service, senior DoCoMo's executive Takeshi Natsuno said at MIDEM.

"Japan has proved what is possible," said Natsuno, the founder and brains behind DoCoMo's I-mode service, insisting that it was not true to say that Japanese people were different or that Japan was a special market. "People in Europe like downloading ringtones and playing games just as much as people in Japan."

"Music and games were the reasons for I-mode's success", Natsuno stressed, as long as prices remained reasonable and the technology easy to use.

Boosted sales from the world of telecoms would be welcome to world of music, which is in dire need of a shot in the arm, with CD sales down 10 percent 2003.

Although the next generation of mobile phones should enable users to download whole tracks with vastly superior sound quality, the music industry is not quite ready to take that turning. Major copyright issues still remain and artists as well as record labels want to be sure the technology is well protected from pirates.

Some executives are also cautioning that ringtones are not the only answer to solving the music industry's doldrums.

"Ringtones are important, but they aren't the big saviour some people think," EMI Music Publishing UK chairman and CEO Peter Reichardt was recently quoted as saying.

Warner music faces $250 million in cuts
Warner Music faces $250M in cuts
By Michael McCarthy, USA TODAY
NEW YORK — The private investor group that is taking over Warner Music Group (Parent: TWX) is expected to move quickly to trim up to $250 million in costs at the struggling music company, say three executives with independent knowledge of the group's plans.
Michelle Branch, a Warner Music Group artist.
By Robert Deutsch, USA TODAY

The group, which includes former Seagram and Universal owner Edgar Bronfman Jr., has hired Boston Consulting Group to help identify areas for cutbacks.

The group is expected to close its $2.6 billion purchase of WMG from Time Warner and take it private this month. WMG ranks as the world's fourth-largest music company with 2002 sales of $4.2 billion and artists such as Madonna, Missy Elliott, Kid Rock, Michelle Branch and the Red Hot Chili Peppers.

The new owners are considering cost-cutting moves ranging from merging labels and dropping artists to layoffs and closing offices. Both Bronfman and the Boston Consulting Group declined to comment.

The new company will be another example of the music business increasingly coming under the thumb of bottom-line-oriented suits rather than free-spending music impresarios. While Bronfman is the public face of the new ownership team as "senior executive," for example, he actually reports to Scott Sperling, managing director of Thomas H. Lee Partners, the buyout firm that's putting up the most cash for the deal. The other buyout firms in the WMG acquisition are Bain Capital and Providence Equity Partners.

This comes as A-list producer Antonio "L.A." Reid recently left as chief of BMG's Arista label in the midst of mounting operating losses and pressure from BMG executives.

Despite having no music experience, former NBC executive Andy Lack was named to run Sony Music Entertainment last year, succeeding Tommy Mottola. Sony Music is planning a 50-50 merger with BMG to create a company called Sony BMG.

"Push has come to shove in the music industry. The excesses of the business won't be tolerated by financially oriented owners," warns Jordan Rohan, music analyst at Schwab Soundview Capital Markets.

Beyond bringing costs into line, the equity groups backing Bronfman also are betting that the struggling $28 billion industry is going to rebound in coming years — that online music sales will work, lawsuits will curb piracy, and new talent will grow sales.

WMG has been on a roll lately, scoring 70 nominations for the 46th Grammy Awards, scheduled for Feb. 8.

But on the way to a rebound, possible cost-cutting at WMG might include:

•Merging record labels. Sperling and Bronfman might merge some WMG record labels to save costs. They include Warner Bros., Elektra and Atlantic Records.

•Dropping acts. In general, the major music companies have been focusing their resources on fewer, bigger-selling artists such as Norah Jones. That would require restructuring the contracts of some lower sellers or dropping them completely. Warner Music International currently has a roster of more than 800 artists performing in 25 languages in 70 countries.

•Layoffs and office closings. Layoffs have already become common across the industry during three years of sales declines. But Rohan estimates the new owners might lay off a third of their global workforce of 5,300 employees. That could include closing some of the 68 WMG offices around the world.

CNN: A look back at the tumultuous year in music.
2003 could go down in history as one of the most controversial years in music ever. Artists got involved in politics, scandal, and a race that ended much like the most recent presidential election.

Things started out on a bad note in February during a Rhode Island nightclub performance by the heavy metal group Great White. The pyrotechnics used in the show started a fire that spread so quickly it killed 100 people. Last month, the group's tour manager and the nightclub's owners were indicted on charges of involuntary manslaughter.

Moving on to March, one of the most popular girl groups, The Dixie Chicks, decided to open their political mouths and bashed President Bush for the U.S.-led war in Iraq. The landslide of criticism that followed could have hurt their careers forever as some radio stations banned their music and record sales fell off the charts. But it didn't hurt for long. The Chicks' 2003 tour was the top seller of the year for country music.

Then it was time to find out who your next "American Idol" would be. Was it really Ruben, or were you "aiken" for Clay. The call for a recount got almost as much attention as the 2000 presidential election. If first-week album sales were to decide, someone would have to ask, "Who counted those votes, Ryan Seacrest or Simon Cowell?"

At this year's MTV Video Music Awards in August, it was the moment nobody saw coming -- the kiss. Britney Spears, the new Queen of Pop, and her idol, Madonna, left us wondering, "Did I see tongue?" Oh, and by the way, Christina Aguilera was involved, too.

The year wrapped up with what may go down as the most controversial story in music ever. Is Michael Jackson guilty? The pop icon was arrested for child molestation in November, and the formal charges were handed down in December. This leaves us to wonder in 2004, Does shamone mean "innocent"?

THE WORLD'S FIRST DEFENSE OF...MADONNA'S AMERICAN LIFE

I'ts really easy to say Madonna's new music stinks. "Madonna's new music stinks!". See-easy! It's much harder to ignore the pervasive, knee-jerk criticism(not to mention dismal sales) of her latest album, American Life, and give it a serious relisten. Sure, it currently resides on several music critics' year-end worst albums list. But aside from it's dumb-ass Madonna-as-che cover art-and the equally dumb-ass rap she does on the title track- it's actually quite a good record that nearly EVERYONE missed because they were gleefully backlashing Ms. Ciccone. To be fair, American Life's secret weapon isn't Madonna herself but her producer, Frenchman Mirwais Ahmadzai. Mirwais' dense, space-agey sound is sometimes a little cold and impersonal-he makes dance music Kubrick would have loved- but it's still good. And it's a nice break from all that top 40 Timbaland/Neptunes rat-a-tat-tat that's starting to sound really repetitive. and now you can listen to DJ's producers and other folks interpret Madonna's(and Mirwais's) work on the new American Life Renixed and Revisited. Take her home in a brown paper bag, play her behind drawn shades, ENJOY!

New Maverick Artist: Ben Jelen.

Music was the constant through Jelen's early life of frequent moves from England to Texas, Scotland, New Jersey, and finally New York. He switched schools often and was never in one place long enough to form his own band, so he practiced the instruments that he could perform solo – piano, guitar, and violin. In high school he started sitting in with friends' bands at occasional gigs. On stage, Ben found a way to connect. He fed on the thrill of having an audience focused on his lyrics and moving to his rhythms. When Jelen (pronounced Yellin) performed, the crowd got excited – people knew they were in on something special. The encouragement his early supporters prompted him to record a demo, which he did late at night while working as a producer and engineer in a local studio.

As Ben's songs took shape, they reflected a nomadic upbringing, a hand-to-mouth existence in New York City, and a free-spirited approach to life. The lucky star that had guided Jelen safely through travels around the world led him to a chance encounter at a Jane's Addiction show.

At the concert, Jelen was approached by a Calvin Klein marketing exec who was scouting for new models. Ben declined his offer to visit a casting audition but the conversation continued, and the CK rep was struck by Ben's charisma and excitement about his new demo. As luck would have it, that executive was Joseph Janus - CEO of New York based artist management firm, Fearless Management. The day after hearing Jelen's demo, Janus signed on as his manager, and the wheels began turning.

Ben was soon seated in front of Guy Oseary - head of Madonna's Maverick Recording Company, and champion of popular artists like Alanis Morissette and Michelle Branch. After a brief performance, Oseary invited Jelen to join the label and he jumped at the chance.

"My musical process is organic and emotional, and Maverick was willing to let me be who I am," Jelen reveals, "I write songs about things that have stirred and inspired me, and I record when my feelings are fresh to preserve those emotions."

Few young artists possess the ability to write, produce, and perform their material, but Jelen was offered the opportunity to do just that. He partnered with a top notch team that included respected producers like The Matrix (Avril Lavigne, Christina Aguilera, Liz Phair) and the Berman Brothers. Legendary mixer, Jack Joseph Puig (No Doubt, Beck, Black Crow) heard Ben Jelen's rough cut only once before moving the singer/songwriter's project to the top of his long waiting list.

“The music industry is full of a lot of mediocracy,” Puig states frankly. “I found Ben and his songs to be compelling and fresh. Ben Jelen is not an accident and will be with us for a while.”

Give It All Away is a pop album that makes creative use of unconventional elements. Banjo, strings, and Celtic sounds are laced throughout dance tracks and ballads alike. Each cut reveals more about this new artist: "Come On" throbs with yearning for a sweetheart, "Slow Down," recalls the innocence of childhood, and “Stay” is the joyful delight at the beginning of new love.

Ben explains the mix of songs on the album, "I don't write just one type of song. I write what I see, do, and feel. The blood boiling when you're angry, your pulse racing when you're excited – that's what's important. When I feel like that, the song already exists in my mind and I just start writing."

Ben Jelen's debut album presents a series of songs that combine passionate vocals with lush instrumentation and upbeat flourishes. "Give It Away" will be released by Maverick Recording Company in March 2004.

Kylie as Britain's longest reigning pop princess.
Kylie Minogue dethroned Madonna as Britain's longest reigning pop princess Monday, Nov. 10, 2003, as measured by the time between first and last chart-toppers, after the Australian singer's latest single went straight to No. 1 in the music charts. The track, "Slow," gave Minogue her seventh No. 1 in Britain - 15 years and nine months after she first hit the top of the charts with "I Should Be So Lucky" in 1988.
Madonna went 15 years and one month between her first British No. 1, 1985's "Into The Groove," and her last, "Music," in 2000, according to officials at British Hit Singles.
